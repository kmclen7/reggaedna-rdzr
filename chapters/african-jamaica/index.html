Chapter 03 · Survival, adaptation, invention

African Jamaica

Kumina, Burru, Jonkonnu, Revival, the Maroons and mento: the living traditions that formed the ground on which reggae would grow.

By the end you can

  • Distinguish the traditions of African Jamaica and their different African sources.
  • Explain why Kumina's Kongo roots date largely to the years after emancipation.
  • Read Hans Sloane's 1707 account as an early musical document.
  • Show how African-derived instruments and rhythms entered mento and later Jamaican pop.

An early document

In 1707 the physician and collector Hans Sloane published an account of a stay in Jamaica two decades earlier. It describes the instruments of enslaved people, including a calabash-bodied, stringed instrument he called a "strum strump", which scholars identify as an early form of the banjo. It also contains two pages of notated music, transcribed on his behalf by a musician he calls Baptiste. This is widely regarded as the earliest notation of African-Caribbean music.

The source is both precious and problematic. It is a European view of African music, and scholars debate who Baptiste was and how faithfully he captured the sound. But it proves that by the late seventeenth century, enslaved Jamaicans were making distinctive music that a visitor thought worth writing down.

Kumina: a Kongo tradition that arrived after slavery

Kumina, centred on the parish of St Thomas in the east, is among the most clearly Kongo-derived traditions in Jamaica. Historian Monica Schuler showed in her study Alas, Alas, Kongo that it grew in large part from Central Africans who arrived in the 1840s and 1860s as indentured labourers, many of them people who had been freed from slave ships by the Royal Navy and then recruited to work in Jamaica.

That detail overturns a common assumption. Not every African-derived tradition came down unbroken from the era of slavery. Some were renewed by a second wave of arrivals after slavery ended. Kumina communities are organised in "nations", and its ceremonies combine drumming, song, dance and spirit possession.

Revival, and the Christianity that Africans remade

Jamaican Christianity was transformed by those who embraced it. The Great Revival of 1860 to 1861 swept the island, and out of it grew Revival Zion and Pukumina (also called Pocomania), religions that combine Bible, prophecy, spirit possession and African-derived ritual. Their ceremonies include a distinctive rhythmic breathing known as "trumping" or "labouring". A generation later, preachers such as Alexander Bedward drew thousands to Kingston.

Rather than a "mixture" of two religions, Revival is better understood as a Jamaican religion in its own right, one in which African and European elements are reinterpreted together.

Maroon heritage

Maroon communities preserved distinct traditions. Researchers such as Kenneth Bilby have documented the ritual music of the Maroons, including "Kromanti" ceremonies and a ritual language with Akan roots. The abeng, an animal-horn instrument, was used as a signal in war. The Maroons of Accompong still gather each year in January to celebrate their history.

The treaties of 1739 and 1740 are sometimes remembered as victories. They also bound the Maroons to return people who fled slavery, a painful complexity that students should not skip.

Jonkonnu, Burru and mento

Jonkonnu is a Christmas masquerade documented in Jamaica from the eighteenth century, with masked figures and drumming, fife and dance. Its origins are debated, and scholars see a blend of West African masquerade and British mumming traditions. Burru, a drumming tradition associated with festivals and with the Kingston poor, is cited by scholars as an African-derived influence on Nyabinghi drumming. We examine that link in Chapter 04.

Mento, Jamaica's first commercially recorded popular music, emerged from this world. Early mento bands combined banjo, guitar and hand drums with a bass instrument called the rumba box, a wooden box with metal tongues that are plucked. Its design is a form of the lamellophone, the family that includes the African mbira, and it carried the bass line long before the electric bass did.

Seldom told

Scholarly interpretation

Kumina's Kongo came after freedom

Schuler's research ties Kumina to Central African indentured labourers who arrived from the 1840s, many freed from slave ships, rather than to the slavery era alone.

Documented historical fact

The earliest notated Caribbean music

Two pages of music in Sloane's 1707 book are widely regarded as the first notation of African-Caribbean music. The transcriber, Baptiste, is thought by some scholars to have been a free person of colour, while the performers were enslaved.

Scholarly interpretation

The bass was a thumb piano

The rumba box that carried the bass in mento is a giant lamellophone, kin to the African mbira. Its route to Jamaica is debated, with some sources pointing to Cuba, but the design is African in lineage.

Documented historical fact

Trumping

Revival worshippers use a rhythmic, guttural breathing called trumping to induce spirit possession, an example of a rhythm made with the body, not an instrument.

Key terms

Kumina
A Kongo-derived Jamaican religion and music tradition centred on St Thomas.
Revival / Pukumina
Jamaican religions combining Christianity and African-derived ritual, with possession and drumming.
Kromanti
Maroon ritual tradition with Akan-derived language and music.
Jonkonnu
A Christmas masquerade with costumed characters, music and dance.
Lamellophone
An instrument whose sound is made by plucking metal or cane tongues, such as the mbira and the rumba box.
Mento
Jamaican folk-popular music of the first half of the twentieth century, with commentary lyrics.

How the music and spiritual traditions of enslaved Africans survived in Jamaica

The story before the story

Every account of reggae that begins in Kingston in the 1960s starts in the middle. The rhythm, the chant, the drum and the faith that feed roots reggae each had a long earlier life, and most of that life was lived under a law that treated African culture as a danger.

This article looks at how that culture survived. It does not claim that any one drum or song crossed the Atlantic unchanged. The evidence points to something harder and more interesting: people from many parts of Africa, thrown together on island estates, kept some things, lost some, and made new ones.

The sources are uneven. Some come from enslavers and colonial officials, who recorded what frightened or amused them. Others come from historians and from communities that still keep the traditions. Where a claim is well supported we say so, and where it is disputed or thin we say that too.

The survival of African music and belief in Jamaica was not an accident of memory. It was a series of choices made under pressure, by people with very little power.

Chapter One

Not One Africa but Many

The peoples who were brought to Jamaica, and why the differences matter

Jamaica was a Spanish colony until the English invasion of 1655, and then a British one [2]. Under Britain it became a sugar island, and around the 1830s roughly 300,000 people were held in slavery there [1][7]. They had been taken from many parts of West and Central Africa.

Three broad groups appear again and again in the sources on Jamaican culture. People shipped from the Gold Coast are usually called Coromantee or Kromanti, a name that comes from Kormantin, a fort and trading post in what is now Ghana [10]. Central Africans, often called Kongo, are the group behind Kumina, which arrived in large numbers late, with indentured labourers after emancipation [12]. Igbo origins are also proposed for some Jamaican traditions, for example the masking of Jonkonnu [4].

The label on a ship's papers was not the same as a person's nation. "Coromantee" in particular described where someone was embarked more than who they were. This is why careful writers speak of traditions with Akan, Kongo or Igbo influences, not of a single African inheritance.

The mix mattered. On an estate, people with different languages, instruments and gods had to find common ground. The Jamaican traditions that followed, Myal, Kumina, Burru, Jonkonnu and the Maroon ceremonies, are best read as new forms made from several African sources, not as copies of one.

What reached Jamaica was a meeting of many African traditions. What grew from it was Jamaican.

Chapter Two

What the Law Tried to Stop

Drums, horns and assemblies under the slave codes

Colonial authorities understood that sound could carry messages and gather crowds. A Jamaican act of 1760, the year of Tacky's War, aimed to prevent rebellion. As summarised in the catalogue of the University of New Brunswick's slavery and freedom laws, it restricted enslaved people from leaving their plantations without tickets and forbade gatherings of people from different estates to beat drums or blow horns, which lawmakers judged to produce dangerous consequences [8].

A page from the University of Cambridge's Legacies of Enslavement project adds that Jamaican law also targeted the sounding of conch shells in assemblies of enslaved people, treating them as military instruments alongside drums and horns [9]. The dating of the later measures is not settled in the sources we found.

Belief was policed as well. Colonial law treated Myal and Obeah as one, and the Obeah Law of 1898 states that the two terms have the same meaning [11][14]. Our pages on Obeah and Myal explain why practitioners saw the two as different and often opposed.

Enforcement was uneven. Our Burru page notes that, according to some sources, Burru drumming was largely tolerated because its steady rhythm suited field labour [13]. That is a reminder that the same drum could be a threat in one setting and an asset in another, and that laws on paper did not always match daily life.

Laws against drums and gatherings show what the authorities feared. They do not show that the drums stopped.

Chapter Three

Music in Plain Sight

Early notation, the banjo's ancestor and the Christmas masquerade

Some African music was written down almost as soon as it was heard. The physician Hans Sloane, who spent about fifteen months in Jamaica in 1687 and 1688 as doctor to the new governor, published his account in 1707, and it includes the music and words of songs, set down with the help of a local musician [5]. Scholars regard two pages of notation in it as the earliest notation of African-Caribbean music, and the transcriber, Baptiste, is thought by some to have been a free person of colour [17]. The book is a European's view and was made by a man tied to the slave economy, so it has to be read critically, but it shows that by the late seventeenth century enslaved Jamaicans were making music distinctive enough to record.

Another surviving form is Jonkonnu, a masquerade held at Christmas, with masks, characters such as the King and Queen and Pitchy Patchy, and music played on goatskin drums and cowbells [4]. Edward Long's History of Jamaica of 1774 is among the early accounts Wikipedia cites [4]. Where it comes from is disputed. Theories point to Akan celebrations, to Igbo masking and yam festivals, to Yoruba Egungun masquerades and to Ga festivals, and even the origin of the name is uncertain [4]. Long was a defender of slavery, and his account has to be read with that in mind.

Jonkonnu matters here for two reasons. It shows African and European elements fused in public performance, and it shows that some African-derived practice was allowed in the open, on terms the authorities could tolerate, while other practice was forced out of sight.

Some traditions survived by being visible and festive. Others survived by being hidden.

Chapter Four

Freedom in the Hills: The Maroons

Communities that kept African ceremony alive beyond the plantation

When the English took Jamaica in 1655, many of the people enslaved by the Spanish escaped into the interior and joined people who had already fled, forming self-governing communities known as Maroons [1][2]. Over the following decades they fought the colonial government in what are called the Maroon Wars.

After years of guerrilla warfare, Governor Edward Trelawny signed a treaty in 1739 that gave the Leeward Maroons under Cudjoe 1,500 acres and a degree of autonomy, in return for defending the colony against invasion and returning runaways. Similar treaties were made with Windward groups in 1740 [2]. In 1795 and 1796 the Second Maroon War ended with the deportation of about 600 Trelawny Town Maroons, first to Nova Scotia and later to Sierra Leone, despite an understanding that they would not be deported [2].

The Maroons are important to this story because they kept ceremonies that were barred elsewhere. UNESCO's record for the Maroon heritage of Moore Town describes Kromanti Play, a set of collective religious ceremonies created by the Moore Town Maroons from several African spiritual traditions, in which dances, songs and particular drumming styles call on ancestral spirits, and in which a language of African derivation is used [3]. UNESCO calls it the foundation of Maroon identity and inscribed the Moore Town heritage on its Representative List in 2008 [3].

The same record warns that decades of missionary opposition have pushed the practice partly underground and that migration is thinning the community [3]. That is a useful corrective to any picture of an unbroken line. Survival was real, and it was also repeatedly threatened, including after freedom.

The Maroons show that African ceremony could survive where people held land and some autonomy. They also show how fragile that survival was.

Chapter Five

A New Faith, Old Spirits

Myal, the Native Baptists and the road to Revival

The early Baptist churches were part of the story. George Liele, a formerly enslaved preacher from Georgia, left America with the British in 1782, arrived in Jamaica, preached at the Kingston racecourse and built a congregation and chapel [6]. Moses Baker, a barber who arrived in 1783, was baptised by Liele [6]. In 1792 Liele wrote the covenant for the Ethiopian Baptists of Jamaica [6], a name that would echo later in Rastafari history.

Black deacons in these congregations had a good deal of independence, which let people shape their own religious life, including how they understood Baptist teaching [7]. Our Myal page describes how the older tradition of spirit mediumship overlapped with them. After emancipation, Schuler says, Myal bands on estates were combining African ways of solving problems with the practices of the Native Baptist churches [11].

The Baptist War shows how far this religion was entangled with the struggle for freedom. From 25 December 1831 to 5 January 1832, a rebellion led by the Black Baptist deacon Samuel Sharpe involved as many as 60,000 of Jamaica's roughly 300,000 enslaved people, the largest slave uprising in the British West Indies [7]. It was crushed, with an estimated 310 to 340 people executed afterwards, Sharpe among them, and white mobs destroyed churches built for Black congregations [7]. Historians argue that the brutality hastened emancipation [7].

After freedom the same pattern continued. Myal mediums led a revitalisation movement in 1841 and 1842, and the far larger revival of 1860 produced the Revival churches in which, our sources say, the Myal spirit persisted as the Holy Spirit [11][15].

Faith, resistance and African spirit practice were not separate threads. In Jamaica they were often woven together.

Chapter Six

After Freedom

New arrivals, new cities and the laws that stayed

Slavery ended in stages. The Slavery Abolition Act passed in 1833, partial emancipation followed in 1834 with an apprenticeship system, and full emancipation came in 1838 [7][1].

Emancipation did not end African arrivals. From the 1840s, indentured labourers came from Central Africa, many of them taken from slave ships, and Monica Schuler's research ties Kumina to them, not to the slavery era alone [12]. This is why Kumina is described as Kongo-rooted and as a more recent import than the Akan-influenced traditions that were already established.

Freedom also drove people to town. Our Burru page describes players moving from the countryside, especially Clarendon, to West Kingston, where they lived in crowded, poor districts and where the music became linked with the urban poor and was sometimes treated with suspicion [13].

The legal pressure on African-derived religion continued. The Obeah Law of 1898 consolidated earlier laws and treated Obeah and Myal as the same offence [14][11]. Colonial attitudes to African culture did not change with the end of slavery.

Freedom was also contested. The Morant Bay rebellion of 1865 was led by a Baptist deacon, Paul Bogle, a reminder that church leaders were still at the centre of resistance a generation after emancipation [1].

Emancipation changed who could be held as property. It did not end the pressure on African culture, and it brought new African arrivals and new cities in which that culture was remade.

Chapter Seven

The Drum Finds a New Home

From Burru and Kumina to the Rastafari gathering

By the twentieth century the elements were all present. There were the three-drum patterns of Burru, with a bass, a funde and a repeater, the sacred drumming and possession of Kumina, the Revival drumming of the churches, and, in West Kingston, a poor, mostly Black population receiving a new message about Africa and Ethiopia [13][12][15].

Wikipedia's account of Nyabinghi says that Rasta drummers developed Nyabinghi drumming by the 1950s as a ritual music influenced by older Afro-Jamaican styles, including Burru and the ritual drumming of Kumina and Revival Zion [16]. Our Burru page records one account that many Burru players joined the movement and that the drumming became Nyabinghi, while noting how much each tradition contributed is disputed [13].

The drummer Count Ossie is the figure most often named in the crossing. He is described as combining Burru drums with the Kumina rhythms of his youth, and his drummers are heard on the Folkes Brothers' "Oh Carolina", produced by Prince Buster around 1960 [13]. That crossing, from a plantation-era drum to a sacred gathering to a record, is the subject of the next article in this collection.

Nyabinghi drumming did not appear from nothing. It grew from Jamaican traditions that had spent generations surviving.

Chapter Eight

What Survived, What Changed, What We Do Not Know

A caution against tidy stories

It is tempting to tell the story as a straight line, from one African drum to one Jamaican drum. The evidence does not support that.

  • Mixed origins. Jamaican traditions blend Akan, Kongo, Igbo and other African sources with European Christian forms [4][12][13].
  • Gaps in the record. Early evidence comes largely from enslavers and officials, such as Sloane and Long, and direct early records of Burru are scarce [13][5][4].
  • Disputed origins. The origin of Jonkonnu, and how much Burru, Kumina and Revival each gave to Nyabinghi, are debated [4][13].
  • Loss as well as survival. Missionary opposition has driven some practices, such as Kromanti Play, partly underground, and migration is thinning communities [3].
  • Unsettled dates. Sources differ on the dates of some laws and treaties, for example the later restrictions on shells [9] and the date of the first Maroon treaty, given as 1738 in one source and 1739 in others [1][2][3].

Survival does not mean sameness. What survived was changed by those who kept it.

Conclusion

The Bridge to Nyabinghi

Between Mother Africa and the first Rastafari grounation lie three centuries of adaptation. People from many African nations were brought to Jamaica in bondage. They were forbidden to drum and gather, and they drummed and gathered. They were told that their spirits were demons or superstition, and they kept their spirits, renamed them or folded them into new churches. They fled to the hills and made ceremonies there. They were freed, and carried what they had into towns.

What the Nyabinghi drummers of Kingston inherited was not a single African tradition but this layered Jamaican one. That is why the story of Nyabinghi, told in the next article, has to begin with a name from East Africa and a drum from Jamaica, and why neither explains the other on its own.

The roots are African. The survival is Jamaican. Both belong to the history of reggae.
