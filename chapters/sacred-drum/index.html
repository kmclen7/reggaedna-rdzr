Chapter 04 · Nyabingi and Nyabinghi

The Sacred Drum

One name, two histories. In East Africa it belongs to a woman and a spirit who resisted empire; in Jamaica, to a drum, a chant and a house of Rastafari. How the name travelled is still an open question.

  • Describe the Jamaican Nyabinghi gathering and its three-drum ensemble.
  • Explain why historians treat the name's journey to Jamaica as unresolved.
  • Place Count Ossie in the history of recorded Nyabinghi drumming.

Nyabingi in the Great Lakes

In the highlands where present-day Uganda, Rwanda and Congo meet, Nyabingi was a spirit, and the name was also borne by a movement of mediums, most of them women, who spoke with the spirit's authority. Historians of the region, among them Elizabeth Hopkins and Iris Berger, studied how Nyabingi mediums offered judgement and healing, and how, as European rule arrived around 1900, the cult became a banner for resistance to colonial authority.

The most famous figure is Muhumuza, a woman from the old Rwandan court whose claims to power after the death of King Rwabugiri (1895) rest on accounts that differ in detail. Sources agree that German authorities detained her around 1908, that she escaped and proclaimed she would drive out the Europeans, that she was captured again around 1911 by joint German and British action, and that she lived on in detention in the Kampala area until the mid-1940s. Her son's claim to the throne was part of her challenge to King Musinga. Treat the exact dates with care; the sources conflict.

Nyabinghi in Jamaica

In Jamaica, Nyabinghi is the oldest and most traditional of the Rastafari houses. Its great gathering, called a grounation or simply a binghi, can last for days. Elders lead worship around a fire. Chanting, Psalms and prayers alternate with drumming. The ensemble of three hand drums has parts often named the bass, the fundeh and the repeater (or akete); the bass keeps a steady heartbeat, the fundeh holds the middle voice, and the repeater improvises over both.

Scholars including Barry Chevannes link the drumming to Jamaican Burru and, more distantly, to Kumina, both African-derived. The Smithsonian Folklife essay on roots reggae makes the same connection. The order's outlook is often described as the most militant among the early Rastafari houses, with commentators noting influence from news of the Mau Mau uprising in Kenya in the 1950s.

The open question: how did the name travel?

It is tempting to draw a straight line from Muhumuza's Nyabingi to Jamaica's Nyabinghi. The evidence does not yet justify it. We can say that the East African tradition existed; that the Jamaican tradition exists; and that they share a name and a theme of resistance to white oppression. What is missing is a well-documented channel between them.

Plausible routes under discussion include the Pan-African press of the 1930s, which reported on both African resistance and the Italian invasion of Ethiopia, and the networks of the Ethiopian World Federation. These are hypotheses. A good student will state them as hypotheses.

Rastafari also offer their own account of the name. A widely repeated interpretation reads it as "death to Black and white oppressors". That interpretation is a faith statement, not a linguistic finding, and deserves to be heard as such.

Count Ossie and the recording of a sacred sound

In Kingston, the drummer Count Ossie (Oswald Williams) gathered musicians at a camp in the Wareika Hills. His Rastafari drummers appear on "Oh Carolina", written by John Folkes and recorded around 1959 to 1961 (sources differ) for Prince Buster, a record often called the first ska record. In 1973 Count Ossie and Cedric "Im" Brooks, with their groups merged as the Mystic Revelation of Rastafari, released the triple album Grounation, whose title piece runs over thirty minutes. Count Ossie died in 1976.

To record a sacred sound is to change it. Some elders welcomed the exposure; some worried about commercialising a ritual. Teachers should present both views.

Persecution and apology

Rastafari endured serious persecution. Leonard Howell's Pinnacle community was raided in 1941 and again in 1954, and in April 1963 the Coral Gardens incident near Montego Bay led to a roundup of Rastafari across the island. In April 2017 Prime Minister Andrew Holness apologised in Parliament for the state's conduct, calling it a grave injustice. Students should read the statement and the Public Defender's report in full.

Seldom told

Disputed claim

The name's journey is unproven

No documented chain links Muhumuza's Nyabingi to Jamaica's Nyabinghi. Both exist and share a name and a theme of resistance. The connection remains a research question.

Scholarly interpretation

A woman at the root of the name

In East Africa, Nyabingi mediums were typically women, and Muhumuza is a woman's name in a story often told about men. In Jamaica, women's roles in Nyabinghi are discussed within the tradition.

Documented historical fact

A camp, a school of drummers

Count Ossie's Wareika Hills camp brought together Rastafari drummers with jazz-trained horn players from the Kingston scene.

Community oral tradition

Heartbeat of three drums

The bass drum of a Nyabinghi ensemble is commonly described as the heartbeat, a metaphor offered by practitioners, not a measurement.

Disputed claim

Mau Mau and the Nyabinghi order

Several commentators suggest news of the Mau Mau rebellion influenced the militant stance of the Nyabinghi order. The extent is debated.

NYABINGHI | The Sacred Drum

From an African spiritual tradition of resistance to the heartbeat of Rastafari and the rise of roots reggae

7 Things You Didn't Know About Nyabinghi, Rasta Today

The rhythm that carries a history

Before Bob Marley carried the message of Rastafari onto the international stage, before Burning Spear sang about Marcus Garvey, and before reggae became the soundtrack of resistance for millions around the world, a different sound was gathering strength in Jamaica.

It came from the beating of drums, the chanting of scripture, and the voices of people determined to recover a connection with Africa.

But the story of Nyabinghi did not begin in a Kingston recording studio. Nor did it begin with the Rastafari movement.

Its name carries a history reaching back to the highlands of East Africa, where spiritual authority and political resistance became intertwined during an era of colonial expansion.

Centuries of African displacement had already transformed the Caribbean. Across generations, enslaved Africans and their descendants preserved, adapted, and reimagined musical and spiritual traditions, creating new cultural expressions in Jamaica.

Out of this complex history emerged a movement that would challenge colonial ideas about African identity.

That movement was Rastafari.

And within Rastafari, Nyabinghi became one of the most recognizable expressions of collective worship, cultural memory, and spiritual resistance.

The sacred drumming would eventually reach far beyond religious gatherings, influencing musicians who helped shape Jamaica's popular music.

To understand Nyabinghi is therefore to explore something greater than the origins of a rhythm.

It is to examine how African history, spiritual belief, Jamaican cultural creativity, and the struggle for liberation became connected through music.

This is the story of Nyabinghi.

Chapter One

Nyabinghi Had an African History Before Rastafari Existed

An ancient name, a history of spiritual authority, and the struggle against domination

To discover the earliest roots of the name Nyabinghi, we must travel thousands of miles from Jamaica to the Great Lakes region of East Africa.

In what is now southwestern Uganda and Rwanda, communities maintained religious traditions associated with a powerful female spiritual presence known as Nyabingi, also written Nyabinghi.

These traditions existed long before the emergence of Rastafari in twentieth-century Jamaica.

Within their East African context, Nyabingi was associated with spiritual authority, protection, fertility, healing, and communication between the living and the spiritual world.

Religious specialists, many of whom were women, served as mediums through whom the spirit was believed to communicate.

They occupied positions of influence within their communities.

To understand why this matters, we must recognize that spirituality in many African societies was not always separated from politics, healing, agriculture, or community leadership.

Religious authority could shape decisions affecting the entire community.

The Nyabingi traditions were particularly significant because they demonstrated the influence women could exercise through religious leadership.

As European colonial powers expanded into East Africa during the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, these spiritual institutions became entangled with political resistance.

The Nyabingi movement would eventually be associated with efforts to challenge established rulers and European colonial control.

The name therefore acquired a political significance extending beyond religious ceremony.

It became associated with people who refused to accept the authority imposed upon them.

But how did this East African name reach Jamaica?

This is where the history becomes more complicated.

Popular accounts sometimes suggest that enslaved Africans carried the Nyabingi tradition directly from East Africa to Jamaica and preserved its name unchanged through generations.

That explanation is not supported by a clear historical chain of evidence.

Researchers have instead examined the circulation of reports about African resistance movements during the 1930s, when Rastafari itself was emerging.

Some historical accounts point to newspaper reports that introduced the name Nyabinghi to Jamaican audiences during this period.

What is certain is that the name eventually acquired an important place within Rastafari vocabulary.

Its meaning evolved within a Jamaican movement already inspired by African liberation, Ethiopian identity, biblical prophecy, and opposition to colonial oppression.

The African Nyabingi tradition and Jamaican Rastafari Nyabinghi are historically connected through the adoption of the name, but they should not be treated as identical religious practices.

That distinction is essential to understanding the real history.

Chapter Two

The Woman Behind the Resistance: Muhumuza and the Nyabingi Movement

An African history that deserves to be remembered

The history of Nyabingi cannot be separated from the women who exercised spiritual and political authority in its name.

One of the most important was Muhumuza, also written Muhumusa.

She emerged as a major political and religious figure in the borderlands of present-day Rwanda and Uganda during the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries.

Her life unfolded during a period of enormous political upheaval.

African kingdoms were confronting internal disputes over authority while European colonial governments expanded their power.

Muhumuza became associated with the Nyabingi spiritual tradition and claimed authority that challenged existing political arrangements.

She mobilized followers and became an important figure in resistance against colonial control.

Her movement represented a challenge not only to European political expansion but also to the local authorities cooperating with colonial administrations.

For colonial governments, such movements were particularly troubling.

They combined spiritual authority with the capacity to organize people politically.

In 1911, Muhumuza's resistance was confronted by colonial forces. She was deported to Kampala and kept under British supervision for the rest of her life [11].

Yet the influence of Nyabingi spiritual movements did not end with her arrest.

Other mediums and leaders continued to invoke Nyabingi in the region.

Muhumuza's story, as far as it can be told

Much of what is known about Muhumuza comes from colonial officials, missionaries and the memory of the Kiga people, and accounts do not always agree. The outline, though, is consistent across the sources used here [11][14][15].

She is said to have belonged to the Rwandan royal circle. Rwandan sources name her Muserakande and describe her as a wife of King Kigeri Rwabugiri, who died in 1895. Another account gives her name as Nyakayoga and says she had been married to Rwabugiri's chosen successor, Rutarindwa. A third writer suggests she may have been a mistress rather than an official wife [11][15].

Rwabugiri's death opened a struggle over the throne. In the coup of 1896, his favourite wife, Kanjogera, had her own son Musinga enthroned and set Rutarindwa aside. Muhumuza is said to have fled north to the borderlands of Mpororo with her young son Biregeya, whom she presented as Rwabugiri's rightful heir [11].

In the north she became known as an umugirwa, a medium through whom the spirit Nyabingi was believed to speak. Many mediums spoke for the spirit and carried messages between worshippers and Nyabingi. Muhumuza is described as going further than most: she claimed to embody the spirit herself, and many followers saw her as Nyabingi returned [11][14][15].

With that authority she gathered followers among the Kiga people. She challenged Musinga's court, told people to bring tribute to her instead, and raised armed bands behind her son's claim. She taught her followers to look for Karinga, a sacred drum tied to Rwandan royal power, and said that finding it would make her son king. She also taught that her followers could not be harmed, and that European bullets would turn to water [11][14][15].

Musinga appealed to the Germans, who backed his court. They arrested Muhumuza in 1908 on a charge of witchcraft and held her at Bukoba, on Lake Victoria. Accounts differ on how that ended: one says she escaped in July 1911, another that she was held until 1910 [11][15].

Back in the borderlands she proclaimed herself queen of Ndorwa, the old kingdom now part of Kigezi, and vowed to drive out the German and British colonisers. One account says about three thousand people lived around her fortified home in southern Kigezi. The same account notes that most of her attacks fell on local chiefs who served the Europeans, not on the Europeans themselves [14].

German and British officers joined forces against her. In late September 1911, a force led by a British captain named Reid attacked her camp at Ihanga hill. One writer describes a battle of about six hours in which around forty of her followers died, while a commentator on the same account thinks it was much shorter. She was captured with a light wound to the foot [11][14][15].

Because her area was not yet formally part of the Uganda Protectorate, the Kigezi administration had no power to try her, and the governor ordered her taken to Kampala. She lived there under British supervision for the rest of her life, known as Nabere, with servants, courtiers and cattle, and she taught Nyabingi rituals to visitors. Officials feared that letting her go home would revive the movement, and a provincial commissioner wrote in 1920 that it would be a serious mistake. She died in 1944 or 1945 [11][14][15].

Her capture did not end the movement. Followers including Ndungutse and Basebya organised another rebellion in northern Rwanda, and other mediums kept acting in Nyabingi's name. One account says her arrest led to the Witch Craft Ordinance of 1912, which outlawed beliefs such as Nyabingi; the direct link to her case is not firmly documented. The movement faded around 1930 as the East African Revival spread Christianity through the region [11][14].

Muhumuza was a real person, with a documented arrest and detention, who led a movement that joined spiritual authority to political resistance. Nothing in these sources shows that she or her movement travelled to Jamaica. The Jamaican link rests on a name, which entered Rastafari talk in the 1930s through a newspaper story.

Was Nyabinghi actually an African queen?

The answer requires historical care.

Some popular Rastafari and modern retellings describe a legendary Queen Nyabinghi who led African warriors against European colonizers.

However, historians distinguish between the spiritual figure Nyabingi, traditions about earlier female rulers, and identifiable historical leaders such as Muhumuza.

The available evidence does not establish that every story about a single warrior queen refers to one verifiable historical woman.

Muhumuza, however, was a real historical leader associated with the Nyabingi religious movement.

Her importance should not be obscured by turning the entire history into an unverified legend.

The documented story of women organizing resistance through spiritual authority is already powerful enough without embellishment.

For ReggaeDNA, this history opens an important question:

How did the name associated with female spiritual authority in East Africa become connected to a predominantly male-led religious movement in Jamaica?

Answering that question requires examining the emergence of Rastafari itself.

Chapter Three

Rastafari Gave Nyabinghi a New Spiritual Identity

From African resistance to the awakening of Jamaica

To understand how Nyabinghi became part of Rastafari, we must first understand the Jamaica in which Rastafari emerged.

During the early twentieth century, Jamaica remained under British colonial rule. Although slavery had officially ended in the nineteenth century, its economic and social consequences continued to shape the lives of Black Jamaicans.

Poverty, racial inequality, limited opportunity, and colonial attitudes toward African heritage created an environment in which new ideas about freedom and identity found an audience.

Among the most influential voices was Marcus Mosiah Garvey, the Jamaican Pan-African leader who encouraged people of African descent to reclaim their history, dignity, and right to self-determination.

Garvey's message challenged the belief that African civilization was inferior to European civilization.

Africa was not something to be forgotten. It was a source of history, identity, and pride.

In 1930, an event thousands of miles away would acquire extraordinary significance among Jamaican religious thinkers.

On November 2, Ras Tafari Makonnen was crowned Emperor Haile Selassie I of Ethiopia.

His coronation was interpreted by emerging Rastafari leaders through biblical prophecy and longstanding traditions of Ethiopianism.

For many early Rastafari, Haile Selassie represented the fulfillment of divine promises concerning Africa and its descendants.

Ethiopia became especially important as a symbol of African sovereignty and spiritual liberation.

The Rastafari movement developed around these convictions, challenging colonial authority and dominant religious interpretations.

The extraordinary arrival of the name Nyabinghi

During the Italian invasion of Ethiopia in 1935, reports about African resistance circulated internationally.

One particularly significant article appeared in The Jamaica Times on December 7, 1935. Written under the name Frederico Philos, it described a supposed secret organization called Nyabinghi and linked it to Haile Selassie.

Scholarly accounts describe the article as propaganda, designed to support European colonial efforts to suppress anti-colonial movements, and not as a reliable account of any real international organization [5][10].

Yet its influence appears to have moved in an unexpected direction.

Instead of discrediting Ethiopia among Jamaican readers, the story contributed to the association of Nyabinghi with African resistance and the emerging Rastafari movement.

The history illustrates how an African name could acquire new meanings as it moved through colonial reporting, political imagination, and Jamaican religious thought [5].

Over time, Nyabinghi came to describe important Rastafari gatherings and a distinctive spiritual tradition.

It also became associated with one of the principal Mansions of Rastafari, commonly called the Nyabinghi Order.

Within this new Jamaican context, the name carried ideas of liberation, spiritual judgment, African identity, and collective worship.

The transformation was profound.

An East African name historically associated with female spirit-medium authority and resistance had become part of a Jamaican religious movement centered on Jah, Ethiopia, and the interpretation of biblical scripture.

Nyabinghi was no longer simply a name associated with Africa. Within Rastafari, it had become an expression of spiritual and cultural resistance to Babylon.

In Rastafari language, Babylon represents systems of oppression, corruption, exploitation, and spiritual captivity.

Nyabinghi gatherings provided a setting in which Rastafari communities could affirm their values, strengthen their identity, and express their hope for liberation.

Chapter Four

The Sacred Drums: Understanding the Heartbeat of Nyabinghi

The rhythm is more than a sound

Few elements of Rastafari culture are as distinctive as Nyabinghi drumming.

Its power lies partly in its simplicity.

A deep drum establishes a steady foundation. Another reinforces the underlying pulse. A third introduces changing rhythmic phrases.

Voices join the percussion.

Chants emerge.

The musicians respond to one another, and the rhythm develops through collective participation.

The effect can be deeply moving.

For many Rastafari practitioners, Nyabinghi drumming is not merely a musical performance. It is an act of worship, thanksgiving, remembrance, and spiritual discipline.

Its significance is experienced through participation rather than observation alone.

The three principal drums

Traditional Jamaican Nyabinghi drumming commonly employs three complementary drum roles.

The bass: The foundation

The bass produces the deepest sound.

Its resonant tone establishes the rhythmic foundation around which the other drums operate.

Rastafari practitioners frequently associate this steady pulse with the heartbeat.

The symbolism conveys continuity, life, grounding, and unity.

The funde: The keeper of time

The funde maintains a regular rhythmic pattern.

Its repeated strokes provide stability, allowing the other instruments to interact without losing the underlying beat.

The funde is essential to the ensemble's collective timing.

It represents the disciplined, recurring pulse of the musical structure.

The repeater: The voice of variation

The repeater introduces rhythmic movement and improvisation.

Its player creates changing patterns that interact with the bass and funde.

Rather than simply repeating a fixed phrase, the repeater can respond to the chanting and to the other musicians.

Together, these instruments produce a layered rhythmic conversation.

Where did these drums come from?

The modern Jamaican Nyabinghi ensemble did not emerge in isolation.

Smithsonian Folkways identifies Burru and Kumina as particularly important African-Jamaican musical influences [2].

These traditions developed within Jamaica over generations, preserving and transforming musical practices associated with African cultural heritage.

Burru drumming was especially important in shaping the three-part ensemble later adopted by Rastafari musicians.

During the 1950s, Nyabinghi drumming developed among Rastafari communities in and around Kingston [10].

The musicians created a sacred musical language rooted in the Jamaican cultural environment while expressing their identification with Africa.

This distinction matters.

The modern Nyabinghi drum ensemble is a Jamaican Rastafari development built upon older African-Jamaican musical traditions.

It should not be described as an unchanged percussion system transported directly from Uganda or Rwanda.

Why is it called the heartbeat?

Within Rastafari spiritual interpretation, the rhythm evokes life, unity, and the relationship between humanity and creation.

Many participants describe the steady drumming as grounding or spiritually uplifting.

These experiences form part of the religious meaning of Nyabinghi.

However, describing the rhythm as the heartbeat of creation is a spiritual metaphor, not a scientific claim that the drums reproduce a universal biological or cosmic frequency.

The significance lies in what the rhythm means to the people who practice it.

A Nyabinghi gathering can unite numerous individuals through shared timing, chanting, and participation.

The musicians do not simply accompany the congregation.

They are part of the congregation.

The rhythm belongs to everyone present.

And this is what distinguishes the sacred drum from an ordinary musical performance.

Chapter Five

A Nyabinghi Gathering Is a Spiritual Assembly, Not a Concert

Inside the tradition of Rastafari worship

Imagine arriving at a Rastafari gathering as evening approaches.

People have traveled from different communities to meet.

Some are preparing food. Others are arranging seating, greeting elders, or speaking quietly with friends.

A discussion begins concerning faith, justice, African history, or the challenges facing the community.

As the evening develops, drumming and chanting become central to the gathering.

There may be prayer, meditation, scripture, communal discussion, and moments of reflection.

The gathering continues into the night.

Unlike a commercial concert, its purpose is not to present entertainers before a paying audience.

The participants are gathered for spiritual and communal reasons.

What is a Grounation?

A Grounation, also called a Groundation, is an important Rastafari gathering that brings people together for worship, reasoning, chanting, drumming, and fellowship.

Some assemblies continue for several days.

They can include ceremonial observances, shared meals, spiritual teaching, and discussions about community life.

The Smithsonian documented such gatherings and their importance to Rastafari during its 1989 Festival of American Folklife.

Researchers described ceremonies in which Rastafari brethren and sistren gathered to praise Jah through drumming, chanting, and collective religious observance [6].

Although customs vary between communities, several elements are particularly important.

Reasoning: The exchange of wisdom

Reasoning is a central feature of Rastafari social and spiritual life.

It involves discussion, reflection, and the exchange of ideas.

Participants may explore biblical passages, African history, political injustice, community responsibilities, or questions concerning personal conduct.

The purpose is not simply conversation.

Within Rastafari, reasoning can be understood as a process of developing consciousness and spiritual understanding.

The spoken word carries particular significance.

Words are believed to shape thought, relationships, and the understanding of reality.

This emphasis on language helps explain why Rastafari developed distinctive expressions such as I and I, livity, and word, sound, and power.

Chanting: Scripture becomes communal expression

Rastafari Nyabinghi chants frequently draw upon biblical Psalms, hymns, and other religious songs.

Through repetition and collective singing, familiar religious language becomes part of a distinctive Rastafari expression.

The chants may concern thanksgiving, righteousness, judgment, African liberation, or the rejection of Babylon.

They are not merely lyrics accompanied by percussion.

For participants, chanting can be an act of devotion.

The ceremonial fire

Fire is an important symbol in certain Nyabinghi gatherings.

Depending on the community, it may represent purification, spiritual judgment, truth, or the rejection of corruption.

The imagery of fire also appears throughout biblical scripture and Rastafari language.

Its meaning must be understood within that religious context.

Not every gathering follows identical ceremonial practices, and fire does not hold precisely the same significance for every Rastafari community.

The significance of unity

Perhaps the most important characteristic of a Nyabinghi gathering is its communal nature.

The gathering strengthens relationships between individuals who share a spiritual and cultural identity.

It creates space for collective memory, religious expression, and the transmission of knowledge across generations.

Children may observe elders chanting and reasoning.

Younger musicians learn the rhythms from experienced drummers.

Community members encounter teachings that connect their lives to Africa, Ethiopia, scripture, and Rastafari history.

In this way, Nyabinghi helps preserve a living tradition.

The drums carry the rhythm, but the community carries the culture.

Chapter Six

Sacred Dates: How Rastafari Keeps Its History Alive

The calendar of remembrance

Nyabinghi gatherings are closely associated with important moments in Rastafari and Ethiopian history.

Unlike purely commercial music festivals, these observances often commemorate events that hold profound religious, historical, and cultural meaning.

They help connect contemporary Rastafari communities with the origins and continuing development of their movement.

Among the most significant dates are the following.

January 7: Ethiopian Christmas

Ethiopian Christmas, known as Genna, is observed on January 7 in most years.

The Ethiopian Orthodox celebration reflects Ethiopia's longstanding Christian heritage.

It also holds significance for some Rastafari communities, although their beliefs and observances should not be confused with those of the Ethiopian Orthodox Church.

April 21: Grounation Day

On April 21, 1966, Emperor Haile Selassie I arrived in Jamaica.

His visit became one of the defining events in Rastafari history.

Large crowds gathered at Kingston's airport to welcome the Ethiopian emperor.

For many Rastafari, the occasion represented a moment of profound spiritual significance.

Rastafari elder Mortimo Planno played a notable role in the events surrounding Selassie's arrival.

The anniversary is commemorated as Grounation Day.

July 23: The birth of Haile Selassie I

Haile Selassie was born on July 23, 1892.

His birthday is an important observance within Rastafari communities that regard him as a central spiritual figure.

August 17: Marcus Garvey's birthday

Born on August 17, 1887, Marcus Garvey became one of the most influential advocates of Black self-determination and Pan-African consciousness.

Although Garvey was not a founder of Rastafari, his ideas contributed significantly to the intellectual and cultural environment from which the movement emerged.

His birthday is commemorated by many Rastafari communities.

November 2: Coronation Day

On November 2, 1930, Ras Tafari Makonnen was crowned Emperor Haile Selassie I of Ethiopia.

For early Rastafari thinkers, the coronation was interpreted through biblical prophecy and ideas about African redemption.

Its significance remains central to Rastafari religious history.

Other important observances include Ethiopian New Year, Emancipation Day, and African Liberation Day.

The specific calendar and forms of observance vary between Rastafari communities [12].

Why these dates matter

Every community needs ways of preserving its collective memory.

Rastafari observances provide opportunities to remember people, events, and ideas that shaped the movement.

Through chanting, storytelling, reasoning, and communal gatherings, historical knowledge is passed from one generation to another.

The calendar therefore does more than mark the passage of time.

It helps preserve the identity of a people and the principles of their faith.

Chapter Seven

From Sacred Drumming to Roots Reggae: How Nyabinghi Helped Shape the Music

The missing connection in reggae history

By the time reggae emerged during the late 1960s, Jamaica had already experienced several major musical transformations.

Mento had developed as a distinctive Jamaican popular and folk music tradition.

American rhythm and blues had influenced the island's recording industry and sound-system culture.

Ska emerged around the late 1950s and early 1960s.

Rocksteady followed during the mid-1960s.

And around 1968, musicians began developing the sound that became known as reggae.

The history is not a straight line in which one style completely replaced another.

These genres overlapped, interacted, and influenced one another.

Within this musical environment, Rastafari and Nyabinghi drumming made important contributions.

Smithsonian research identifies Nyabinghi as one of the musical traditions that influenced Jamaican popular music, including early ska and later reggae [1].

But the most important figure connecting sacred Rastafari drumming with commercial Jamaican recordings was a musician whose contributions deserve far wider recognition.

His name was Count Ossie.

Count Ossie: The bridge between the sacred drum and Jamaican popular music

Born Oswald Williams, Count Ossie became one of the most influential drummers associated with early Rastafari music.

His community in the Wareika Hills near Kingston was an important gathering place for musicians and Rastafari practitioners.

There, drumming was not treated simply as entertainment.

It was connected to communal identity, spiritual discipline, and African cultural consciousness.

Count Ossie helped bring that sound into the recording studio.

Around 1960, he and his drummers participated in the recording of "Oh Carolina" by the Folkes Brothers, produced by Prince Buster.

The recording became a landmark because it brought Rastafari drumming into Jamaican popular music at a time when the movement remained marginalized by much of mainstream society.

The musical importance of the record was considerable.

It demonstrated that the sound associated with Rastafari gatherings could form part of commercially recorded Jamaican music.

Count Ossie's contribution helped establish a connection between sacred drumming and the emerging popular music industry [13].

Mystic Revelation of Rastafari

Count Ossie later became associated with the influential ensemble Mystic Revelation of Rastafari.

Its remarkable album Grounation, released in 1973, brought together Nyabinghi drumming, chanting, jazz influences, and extended musical performance.

The album remains an important recording for understanding the relationship between Rastafari spirituality and musical innovation.

Rather than presenting Nyabinghi as merely an influence hidden beneath reggae, Grounation allowed listeners to experience a substantial musical expression of the Rastafari tradition itself.

This distinction is important.

Nyabinghi is not simply an early version of reggae.

It is an independent sacred musical tradition that influenced aspects of Jamaican popular music.

Bob Marley and the worldwide spread of Rastafari consciousness

Bob Marley was not the inventor of reggae.

Nor was he the inventor of Nyabinghi drumming.

His importance lies in the extraordinary way he helped carry reggae and Rastafari-associated ideas to an international audience.

Marley's musical development began within Jamaica's ska and rocksteady eras.

With Peter Tosh and Bunny Wailer, he became part of a generation of musicians who transformed Jamaican popular music.

As Rastafari became increasingly central to Marley's worldview, its language and spiritual themes appeared more prominently in his recordings.

One particularly important example is "Rastaman Chant," released on the Wailers' 1973 album Burnin'.

The recording draws on Rastafari chanting traditions and offers listeners a musical expression different from conventional reggae songwriting.

Its importance is not simply that it contains percussion or religious language.

It demonstrates how Rastafari musical culture could become part of internationally distributed popular music.

Burning Spear and the memory of Africa

Winston Rodney, known as Burning Spear, became another major voice of roots reggae.

His recordings repeatedly explored Marcus Garvey, African identity, slavery, historical memory, and liberation.

Songs such as Marcus Garvey and Slavery Days helped make African and Jamaican history central subjects within reggae.

Burning Spear's work illustrates a defining feature of roots reggae.

The music was not concerned solely with romance or entertainment.

It could serve as a means of historical remembrance and political expression.

Peter Tosh and the language of resistance

Peter Tosh brought a particularly uncompromising political voice to reggae.

His music addressed racial injustice, human rights, equality, and opposition to oppressive institutions.

Although his musical style differed from Nyabinghi chanting, his work shared important themes with Rastafari traditions of resistance to Babylon.

The influence continues

The connections between Rastafari spirituality and Jamaican music did not end with the roots reggae generation of the 1970s.

Artists including Luciano, Sizzla, Anthony B, Capleton, and later roots revival performers helped bring Rastafari language, biblical imagery, African consciousness, and social commentary into new musical settings.

Capleton, for example, emerged from the dancehall tradition before becoming closely associated with Rastafari-oriented conscious music.

His recordings frequently use the imagery of fire, judgment, purification, and resistance.

These themes reflect aspects of Rastafari religious language, although his musical style should not be mistaken for traditional Nyabinghi ceremony.

Similarly, modern musicians may incorporate Nyabinghi-inspired percussion or chants into roots reggae, dub, and contemporary productions.

Each generation reinterprets the relationship between spirituality and music.

Did reggae's famous one-drop rhythm come directly from Nyabinghi?

This question deserves a careful answer.

The one-drop rhythm is strongly associated with reggae drumming, particularly the emphasis created by the kick drum and snare or rimshot within the rhythmic cycle.

It is sometimes described as a direct descendant of the Nyabinghi heartbeat.

There are genuine historical and musical connections between Nyabinghi and reggae, but reggae's rhythmic development also reflects ska, rocksteady, American rhythm and blues, jazz, mento, and Jamaican studio innovation.

It would therefore be misleading to claim that the one-drop was simply copied from Nyabinghi.

The more accurate explanation is that Nyabinghi contributed to reggae's broader musical and spiritual identity while reggae developed through several interacting traditions.

That history is richer than a single-origin story.

It reveals how Jamaican musicians combined inherited cultural practices with new creative possibilities.

Chapter Eight

The Spiritual Meaning of Liberation: Why Nyabinghi Remains Important

Freedom of the people, freedom of the mind

The significance of Nyabinghi extends beyond its contribution to reggae.

For many Rastafari practitioners, its deepest meaning concerns liberation.

But what does liberation mean within this tradition?

The answer has several dimensions.

Liberation from oppression

Rastafari emerged within a society shaped by colonialism, racial inequality, and the enduring consequences of slavery.

Its language of Babylon challenged institutions and social arrangements regarded as unjust.

Nyabinghi gatherings provided communities with a space to affirm their spiritual independence and shared identity.

The tradition helped connect religious expression with ideas of social justice and African liberation.

Liberation through knowledge

Knowledge of African history occupies an important place in Rastafari consciousness.

For generations, colonial education often presented African civilizations through distorted or dismissive narratives.

Rastafari challenged those narratives.

It encouraged people of African descent to question inherited assumptions about history, religion, beauty, language, and identity.

Within this framework, spiritual liberation was connected to the recovery of historical knowledge.

Understanding Africa became part of understanding oneself.

Liberation through community

A Nyabinghi gathering also creates opportunities for communal support.

Participants share food, ideas, religious observances, and cultural knowledge.

Elders transmit teachings.

Musicians teach rhythms.

Younger members gain access to traditions that connect them with earlier generations.

In this setting, liberation can also mean freedom from isolation.

The gathering reminds participants that they belong to a community with a shared history and spiritual purpose.

Liberation through spiritual discipline

Within Rastafari, the concept of livity concerns the way spiritual convictions are expressed through everyday living.

Livity may involve dietary practices, meditation, religious study, ethical conduct, attitudes toward nature, and relationships with other people.

Nyabinghi can form part of this wider religious discipline.

Its significance is not confined to a particular gathering.

The principles explored through chanting and reasoning may shape how participants understand their responsibilities beyond the ceremonial space.

Is Nyabinghi intended to heal?

Many Rastafari practitioners describe Nyabinghi chanting and drumming as spiritually strengthening, cleansing, or healing.

These descriptions express religious and personal experiences.

They should be respected as part of the tradition's meaning.

At the same time, such experiences should not be presented as scientific proof that a particular drum frequency removes illness, negative energy, or psychological distress.

The value of Nyabinghi to its participants may include spiritual reassurance, community support, cultural belonging, and emotional expression.

These experiences do not need exaggerated supernatural or medical claims to be meaningful.

Nyabinghi offers a way for Rastafari communities to connect remembrance, religious conviction, and collective identity through living cultural practice.

Chapter Nine

Understanding the Spiritual Difference Between African Nyabingi and Rastafari Nyabinghi

Two traditions connected by a name, but separated by history and theology

One of the most important responsibilities of ReggaeDNA is to explain religious history without confusing different belief systems.

The East African Nyabingi traditions and Jamaican Rastafari Nyabinghi developed within different societies and spiritual frameworks.

In East Africa, Nyabingi was associated with a female spiritual presence and religious practices involving mediums.

In Jamaica, Nyabinghi became associated with Rastafari worship, biblical chanting, drumming, and the religious significance of Haile Selassie I.

The two traditions are not interchangeable.

African Nyabingi spirituality

In its historical East African setting, Nyabingi beliefs involved relationships between human communities and the spiritual world.

Mediums were believed to communicate with the spirit.

The tradition could be associated with protection, healing, social authority, and political resistance.

These practices belonged to particular African societies and should not be generalized as representative of every African religion.

Rastafari spirituality

Rastafari developed through a different historical process.

It drew on biblical scripture, Ethiopianism, Pan-African thought, African identity, and the particular experiences of Black Jamaicans under colonial rule.

Many Rastafari regard Haile Selassie I as divine or as a manifestation of Jah, although interpretations vary.

Nyabinghi gatherings commonly involve devotion to Jah through chanting, drumming, prayer, and reasoning.

Unlike the East African spirit-medium traditions, Rastafari Nyabinghi is not fundamentally organized around communication with a female possessing spirit.

Christianity and Rastafari

Rastafari uses biblical language extensively, but its theological interpretations can differ substantially from those of mainstream Christian denominations.

For example, the identification of Haile Selassie with divinity is not accepted within mainstream Christian doctrine.

Ethiopian Orthodox Christianity also has its own theology, liturgy, historical institutions, and religious traditions.

It should not be treated as identical to Rastafari simply because Ethiopia holds a central place in both histories.

Why these distinctions matter

Understanding a religion does not require accepting all its theological claims.

A historian can study the social importance of Nyabinghi without deciding whether a spiritual presence exists.

A Christian reader can examine Rastafari beliefs while maintaining a different understanding of God.

A Rastafari practitioner can explain the sacred meaning of Nyabinghi from within their own tradition.

ReggaeDNA should make space for these perspectives while clearly identifying which statements are historical evidence and which are matters of faith.

Chapter Ten

The Living Legacy: Why Nyabinghi Still Matters Today

The sacred drum in a changing world

More than six decades after Rastafari drumming began entering Jamaican commercial recordings, Nyabinghi remains an important expression of Rastafari religious and cultural life.

Its significance cannot be measured solely by its influence on reggae.

For participating communities, it represents continuity.

It preserves religious teachings, ceremonial practices, and collective relationships.

It keeps alive aspects of a cultural movement that challenged the social and racial assumptions of colonial Jamaica.

Its music has also traveled internationally.

Rastafari communities now exist far beyond Jamaica, and Nyabinghi-associated chanting and drumming can be encountered in different parts of the African diaspora and beyond.

Meanwhile, reggae itself has developed into a global musical language.

Jamaican musicians have influenced artists throughout Africa, Europe, the Americas, and Asia.

Yet the worldwide popularity of reggae can sometimes obscure the distinctive histories that contributed to its creation.

Listeners may recognize the offbeat guitar, the deep bass, or the characteristic drum patterns without knowing the cultural movements associated with the music.

They may recognize Bob Marley's image without understanding Marcus Garvey, Ethiopianism, or the early Rastafari community.

They may listen to Capleton or Burning Spear without recognizing the differences between the Rastafari mansions or the historical meaning of Babylon.

And they may hear Nyabinghi chanting without knowing that the name has a complex African history reaching beyond Jamaica.

This is why historical preservation matters.

Nyabinghi is not merely a chapter in the history of reggae.

It is a tradition with its own religious identity, musical practices, and community history.

Its relationship with reggae is important precisely because it demonstrates how sacred cultural expression can influence popular music without losing its independent significance.

Conclusion

Before the World Heard Reggae, There Were Stories Waiting to Be Told

The history of Nyabinghi connects places and experiences that might initially appear far removed from one another.

  • The highlands of East Africa.
  • The struggles of women who exercised spiritual and political authority.
  • The colonial transformation of African societies.
  • The forced migration of Africans to the Caribbean.
  • The survival and reinvention of African-Jamaican cultural traditions.
  • The rise of Marcus Garvey's Pan-African message.
  • The coronation of Haile Selassie.
  • The birth of Rastafari.
  • The drumming communities of Kingston.
  • Count Ossie's groundbreaking recordings.
  • The rise of roots reggae.
  • And the worldwide influence of musicians whose songs carried messages of African identity, spirituality, and resistance.

These histories are connected, but they are not all parts of one uninterrupted tradition.

African Nyabingi did not become reggae simply by crossing the Atlantic.

Nor did Rastafari create Jamaican music from nothing.

Instead, different histories converged in Jamaica, where generations of musicians and communities transformed inherited cultural knowledge into new forms of religious and artistic expression.

That creative transformation is one of the most remarkable achievements in modern musical history.

To hear reggae with a deeper understanding is to recognize the many people who contributed to its development.

  • The African communities whose traditions shaped the Caribbean.
  • The enslaved people and their descendants who preserved and transformed cultural practices.
  • The religious leaders who challenged colonial ideas.
  • The drummers who developed new musical expressions.
  • The producers and musicians who brought those sounds into recording studios.
  • And the artists who helped carry Jamaican music across the world.

Reggae did not emerge from one drum, one religion, one artist, or one historical event.

It grew from the experiences of generations.

Nyabinghi is one significant part of that extraordinary history.

Its name recalls African spiritual resistance.

Its Jamaican ceremonies reflect the religious creativity of Rastafari.

Its drumming helped influence the sound and identity of Jamaican popular music.

And its continued practice reminds us that some of the cultural traditions associated with reggae existed before the genre and remain alive independently of its commercial success.

For the next generation, this history offers something more valuable than mythology.

It offers an opportunity to understand how music, memory, spirituality, and identity can shape one another.

The roots are African. The musical transformation is Jamaican. The legacy belongs to a worldwide cultural history.
