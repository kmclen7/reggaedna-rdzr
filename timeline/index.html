Interactive timeline

Four centuries, countless stories.

Follow African history, Jamaican society, Rastafari and music side by side. Each entry carries a label for the kind of claim it makes.

Entries are short summaries. Where a source is linked beneath an entry, that is what it rests on.

  1. c. 4000–600 BCE

    The Archaic Age in the Caribbean

    Archaic Age peoples reach islands of the Caribbean roughly 6,000 to 7,000 years ago. No evidence of Archaic or earlier settlement has yet been found in Jamaica itself, which archaeologists think was not settled until about 600 CE.

    Scholarly interpretationJamaica · Heritage#link

    The heritage list this entry answers gives the Archaic Age as c. 4000 to 600 BCE for the region. For Jamaica the evidence is absent, and a find of earlier settlement would be new.

    Source: Wikipedia: TaínoSimon Fraser University thesis (2022), summarised by search

  2. c. 500 BCE onward

    Ceramic Age peoples in the wider Caribbean

    Ceramic Age peoples enter the Caribbean from north-eastern South America about 2,500 years ago, and later cultures such as the Ostionoid spread across the Greater Antilles. Jamaica is reached much later.

    Scholarly interpretationJamaica · Heritage#link

    The Saladoid label used for this period elsewhere is not covered by the sources read for this page.

    Source: Wikipedia: TaínoWikipedia: Pre-Columbian Jamaica

  3. c. 600–650 CE

    Redware: the first known settlers of Jamaica

    People of the Ostionoid culture, called Redware in Jamaica, settle the island. Early sites include Little River in St Ann and Alligator Pond in Manchester. They are thought to have come from Hispaniola or South America.

    Scholarly interpretationJamaica · Heritage#link

    Sources give 600, 650 or "about 600 to 650"; the place they came from is "likely", not certain.

    Source: Wikipedia: Pre-Columbian JamaicaWikipedia: History of Jamaica

  4. c. 800–950 CE

    Meillacan peoples

    The Meillacan people settle the coast and interior, either absorbing the Redware culture or living beside it. Together they become the Western, or sub-, Taíno.

    Scholarly interpretationJamaica · Heritage#link

    Wikipedia gives c. 950 CE; a Gleaner article gives 800 to 950. Wikipedia's History of Jamaica gives c. 800 CE for Taíno arrival; sources differ.

    Source: Wikipedia: Pre-Columbian JamaicaThe Gleaner: Charting the journey of Jamaica's indigenous ceramic pottery

  5. c. 1200 CE

    Taíno culture

    Taíno culture develops on the island, with villages of circular houses, each holding 10 to 15 families, around a central plaza, and ball courts. The highest published population estimate is 600,000, which is disputed.

    Scholarly interpretationJamaica · Heritage#link

    Population figures are contested estimates, not counts.

    Source: Wikipedia: Pre-Columbian JamaicaWikipedia: Taíno

  6. 5 May 1494

    Columbus lands; Spanish heritage begins

    Columbus lands in Jamaica on his second voyage. In 1503 he is stranded at St Ann's Bay after a storm and leaves in June 1504. A map of 1507 labels the island "Jamaiqua". The Spanish heritage runs to 1655, though the Institute of Jamaica dates formal Spanish control from 1508.

    Documented historical factJamaica · Heritage#link

    Source: Wikipedia: History of JamaicaNational Museum Jamaica (Institute of Jamaica): The Europeans

  7. 1509–1534

    Sevilla la Nueva and Spanish Town

    Juan de Esquivel founds Sevilla la Nueva in 1509, and in 1534 the capital moves to Villa de la Vega, today Spanish Town. Spanish rule brings cattle and sugar cultivation and the destruction of the Taíno population.

    Documented historical factJamaica · Heritage#link

    Source: Wikipedia: History of JamaicaNational Museum Jamaica (Institute of Jamaica): The Europeans

  8. 1494–1655

    Jewish heritage: the first Jews

    The first Jews arrive during the Spanish occupation, many of them conversos fleeing the Inquisition, and some from 1509. They live as Christians in public. The Institute of Jamaica dates refuge from the sixteenth century.

    Scholarly interpretationJamaica · Heritage#link

    Source: Wikipedia: History of the Jews in JamaicaNational Museum Jamaica (Institute of Jamaica): The Europeans

  9. 1513

    African heritage begins: the first enslaved Africans

    The most widely cited date for the first enslaved Africans reaching Jamaica, landing at Sevilla la Nueva. By 1523 about 500 are imported each year. African heritage becomes the largest cultural influence on the island.

    Documented historical factJamaica · Heritage · Africa#link

    One source gives 1534, when 30 Africans were brought from the Canary Islands; 1513 is more widely cited. "Largest cultural influence" is the description in the heritage list used for this page.

    Source: Wikipedia: Immigration to JamaicaWikipedia: History of Jamaica

  10. Early 1600s

    The Taíno decline

    Early historians describe the Taíno as extinct by 1602, and the island's population is about 3,000 in the early 1600s. Descendant communities such as the Yamaye Guani Taíno, and the Maroons of Moore Town, claim Taíno ancestry, and sources describe intermingling in the Blue Mountains.

    Scholarly interpretationJamaica · Heritage#link

    The extinction claim and claims of descent are both contested.

    Source: Wikipedia: History of JamaicaWikipedia: Taíno

  11. 1655

    England takes Jamaica from Spain

    An English expedition seizes Jamaica. Under British rule the island becomes one of the most economically important sugar colonies, built on the labour of enslaved Africans.

    Documented historical factJamaica · Politics#link
  12. May 1655

    British heritage begins: the English take Jamaica

    About 7,000 English troops land near Spanish Town in May 1655, where fewer than 3,000 people live. Jamaica stays British until independence on 6 August 1962. The Treaty of Madrid gives England formal possession in 1670.

    Documented historical factJamaica · Heritage#link

    Source: Wikipedia: History of JamaicaNational Museum Jamaica (Institute of Jamaica): The Europeans

  13. From 1655

    Irish heritage

    The first Irish arrive after the English capture of Jamaica in 1655, many as indentured servants.

    Scholarly interpretationJamaica · Heritage#link

    Wikipedia cites a timeline site for 1655. A claim that two-thirds of the 17th-century European population was Irish rests on a weak source and is not repeated here.

    Source: Wikipedia: Immigration to Jamaica

  14. 1655–1704

    Jewish heritage under English rule

    After 1655 conversos begin to practise Judaism openly. In 1660 Charles II confirms the English citizenship granted to Jews by Cromwell. In 1704 the Sephardic synagogue Kahal Kadosh Neveh Shalom is consecrated in Spanish Town.

    Documented historical factJamaica · Heritage#link

    Source: Wikipedia: History of the Jews in Jamaica

  15. 7 June 1692

    Port Royal is destroyed by earthquake

    An earthquake destroys much of Port Royal, a port town of merchants, including Jewish merchants, and of buccaneers, who had been invited there in 1657.

    Documented historical factJamaica · Heritage#link

    Source: Wikipedia: History of Jamaica

  16. c. 1650–1745

    Scottish influence

    Scots arrive first as deportees under Cromwell, then in the 1690s and after as survivors of the failed Darien colony, with Highlanders from Argyll and refugees from the 1715 and 1745 Jacobite risings. By the mid-1700s about a third of the white population is estimated to be of Scottish origin, and by the late 1700s a third of the plantations are said to be Scottish-owned.

    Scholarly interpretationJamaica · Heritage#link

    The proportions are estimates from secondary sources.

    Source: Dobson: Scots in Jamaica, 1655–1855 (book description)Glasgow Life: Glasgow plantation owners in Jamaica

  17. 1707

    Sloane publishes the first notated African-Caribbean music

    Hans Sloane's account of Jamaica includes two pages of musical notation, transcribed by a musician he calls Baptiste, and a description of the 'strum strump', an early banjo. It is widely regarded as the earliest notation of African-Caribbean music.

    Documented historical factMusic · Jamaica · Africa · Culture#link
  18. 1739–1740

    Maroon treaties

    After decades of armed resistance, the British sign treaties with the Leeward Maroons (1739) and Windward Maroons (1740), recognising communities founded by people who escaped slavery. Maroon music and ceremony are a distinct strand of Jamaican heritage.

    Documented historical factJamaica · Africa · Culture · Politics · Heritage#link
  19. 7 Dec 1754

    Moravian missionaries arrive

    Three Moravian missionaries from England, Zecharias Caries, Thomas Shallcross and Gottlieb Haberecht, land on the St Elizabeth coast and base themselves at the Bogue estate, invited by planters to teach the enslaved. The Moravian mission later opens the Lititz school, called Jamaica's first primary school of any kind, and founds Bethlehem Moravian College in 1861.

    Documented historical factJamaica · Heritage#link

    Wikipedia gives 7 December 1754 and says the missionaries came from England; another source gives 9 December at Black River. The mission worked with and among enslaved people on estates owned by those who invited it.

    Source: Wikipedia: Jamaica Province of the Moravian ChurchThe Gleaner (2016): Moravians in Jamaica, 1754 to 1834

  20. 1760

    Tacky's War

    An Akan man known as Tacky leads a large uprising in St Mary parish. Colonial writers link it to obeah, and the island legislates against the practice (sources differ on the statute's exact date).

    Documented historical factJamaica · Africa · Spirituality · Politics#link
  21. 1787–1831

    Jewish heritage: toward full rights

    The Ashkenazi congregation builds Shangare Yosher in Kingston in 1787, and a fire in 1815 drives many Jews from Port Royal to Kingston. Jews win full political rights in 1831.

    Documented historical factJamaica · Heritage#link

    Source: Wikipedia: History of the Jews in Jamaica

  22. 1791–1803

    French refugees from Saint-Domingue

    After the slave revolt in Saint-Domingue begins in 1791, about 4,000 people seek refuge in Jamaica, by one scholar's estimate. Authorities sort arrivals into "emigrants", mostly white planters and officials who receive lodging money, and "prisoners of war", a wide group that includes civilians. A British Alien Act of 1793 and later measures allow surveillance and expulsion, and in 1803 an act allows summary expulsion of suspicious foreigners.

    Scholarly interpretationJamaica · Heritage#link

    The 4,000 figure is attributed to Wright and Debien; counts shifted year to year and are disputed. Wikipedia says French immigration began in 1791 without a source.

    Source: Age of Revolutions (2023): Confusing labels, French emigrants and prisoners of war in JamaicaItinerario: The categorisation of French refugees in Jamaica

  23. 1807

    British abolition of the slave trade

    Britain outlaws its transatlantic slave trade. Slavery itself continues in Jamaica for another generation, and African-born people remain a significant part of the enslaved population for years afterwards.

    Documented historical factAfrica · Jamaica · Politics · Heritage#link
  24. 1831–1832

    The Baptist War

    Deacon Samuel Sharpe leads a mass strike that grows into the largest uprising in the island. He is executed in 1832; the rebellion helps push Britain towards abolition.

    Documented historical factJamaica · Politics · Spirituality#link
  25. 1834–1838

    Emancipation and apprenticeship

    Slavery is abolished across the British Empire from 1 August 1834, but most formerly enslaved people in Jamaica are bound to an apprenticeship system. Full freedom follows in 1838. African-derived spiritual and musical practices continue to change in free villages and churches.

    Documented historical factJamaica · Politics · Culture · Heritage#link
  26. 1834–1835

    German heritage

    German indentured labourers begin to arrive in 1834, with the first group said to have sailed from Bremen in May. In 1835 the community of Seaford Town is founded in Westmoreland with 249 settlers. Many die in the first weeks and many leave for the United States; Seaford Town is the last remaining German enclave.

    Documented historical factJamaica · Heritage#link

    Accounts of the settlers' fate vary.

    Source: National Museum Jamaica (Institute of Jamaica): The EuropeansMoon Jamaica: Seaford Town

  27. 1834–1838

    Portuguese heritage

    After emancipation a few hundred Portuguese arrive among about 4,000 British, German and Portuguese immigrants under a scheme to obtain white labour. The scheme fails because of high sickness and death. Most Portuguese are thought to have come from Madeira, with a smaller number from the Azores and mainland Portugal. Another group is said to be hired by large estates, mainly in the west, in 1846.

    Needs investigationJamaica · Heritage#link

    This page is the only Jamaica-specific source found; its figures and the 1846 date are uncited, and "not a lot is known". Madeiran migration to Guyana, Trinidad and St Vincent is far better documented. Worth checking against Jamaican archive records. The Portuguese Jewish presence in Spanish Town, said to begin around 1530, is separate and also weakly sourced.

    Source: My Island Jamaica: Portuguese in Jamaica (Wellesley Gayle)Wikipedia: Immigration to Jamaica

  28. 1841–1865

    Central Africans arrive as indentured labourers

    Several thousand West and Central Africans, many freed from slave ships by the Royal Navy, are brought to Jamaica as indentured workers. Historian Monica Schuler links this migration to the Kongo roots of Kumina.

    Scholarly interpretationJamaica · Africa · Culture · Spirituality · Heritage#link
  29. 10 May 1845

    Indian heritage: the first indentured Indians

    The first indentured labourers from India land at Old Harbour Bay, St Catherine, on 10 May 1845, with 200 men, 28 women and 33 children reported. Indenture continues until 1917, bringing more than 36,000 Indians, about two-thirds of whom stay.

    Documented historical factJamaica · Heritage#link

    Most sources name the ship Blundell; one names Maidstone. Passenger counts differ between sources. Wikipedia credits Indian arrivals with cannabis and the chillum pipe and with roti and curry goat, now part of national cuisine.

    Source: Wikipedia: Indo-JamaicansThe Gleaner, 4 May 2026: Jamaica marks 180 years of the arrival of East IndiansWikipedia: Immigration to Jamaica

  30. 1850–1885

    Jewish heritage: consolidation

    The Ashkenazi congregation Rodphei Zadek merges with a Sephardic congregation in 1850. The Shaare Shalom synagogue is first built in Kingston in 1885, and Progressive Judaism enters the community in the early 1900s.

    Documented historical factJamaica · Heritage#link

    Source: Wikipedia: History of the Jews in Jamaica

  31. 1854

    Chinese heritage: the first Chinese arrivals

    Two ships bring Chinese migrants in 1854, one direct from China and one carrying onward migrants from Panama. Contracts last five years and are often violated. About 680 more arrive in 1884. Most are Hakka.

    Documented historical factJamaica · Heritage#link

    One source names the ship Epsom, with 224 people; another names a different ship. Details of the ships differ.

    Source: Wikipedia: Chinese JamaicansWikipedia: Immigration to Jamaica

  32. 1869–1898

    Cuban exiles in Jamaica

    During Cuba's wars of independence, exiles base themselves in Jamaica, close enough for a quick return to Cuba. Studies of the exile community conclude that it supported the independence cause and helped shape a distinct Cuban national identity.

    Scholarly interpretationJamaica · Heritage#link

    Not confirmed: summarised from Spanish-language scholarship that has not been read in full. Numbers of exiles are not given.

    Source: Islas (Universidad Central de Las Villas): study of Cuban emigration in Jamaica, 1869 to 1898 (Spanish; read only through a search summary)

  33. 1879–1906

    Indian heritage: repatriation ends

    Money grants for returning to India end in 1879 and land grants are abandoned in 1906. Many Indians stay. Indenture ends in 1917.

    Documented historical factJamaica · Heritage#link

    Source: Wikipedia: Indo-Jamaicans

  34. 1880s–1914

    Heritage abroad: work in Panama and beyond

    The Jamaican diaspora begins with work abroad, including labour on the Panama Canal in the 1880s and 1910s, and migration to Cuba and Central America. The diaspora later grows to about two million people of Jamaican descent abroad.

    Scholarly interpretationJamaica · Heritage#link

    Wikipedia's diaspora article is flagged for citations; its UK figures conflict (400,000 in the infobox, about 800,000 in the text).

    Source: Wikipedia: Jamaican diaspora

  35. Late 1800s

    Arabs | Syrian and Lebanese heritage

    Arab Jamaicans are Jamaicans of Syrian, Lebanese or Palestinian origin or descent, wholly or in part. The first families came in the later nineteenth century from the Ottoman Levant, most of them Christians. Unlike the Chinese and Indian migrants, they were not indentured: they chose to leave, often to escape religious and political pressure, and many followed relatives who had gone before. Most began as door-to-door peddlers, then opened dry goods shops in downtown Kingston, and the community rose to prominence in trade, tourism and public life. Its members include the record producer Ken Khouri and the former prime minister Edward Seaga.

    Scholarly interpretationJamaica · Heritage · Music#link

    Wikipedia's Arab Jamaicans article says the migrants were brought by European colonialists; the Gleaner history pages say they came of their own free will, and we follow that better-sourced account. Wikipedia's Immigration to Jamaica says the community was established in the early 19th century, while Jamaican sources say the late 1800s or 1890s. The sources differ.

    Source: Wikipedia: Arab JamaicansThe Gleaner: Lebanese and Syrians in Jamaica (history pages)Institute of Jamaica: The Syrians and LebaneseWikipedia: Immigration to Jamaica

  36. 1896

    Ethiopia wins at Adwa

    An Ethiopian army defeats an invading Italian force at Adwa, a rare decisive victory of an African state over a European colonial power, and a touchstone for later Pan-African thought.

    Documented historical factAfrica · Politics#link
  37. 1908–1911

    Muhumuza and the Nyabingi resistance

    In the Great Lakes region of East Africa, the name Nyabingi is tied to spirit-medium authority and anti-colonial resistance. Muhumuza, a woman from the Rwandan court, was detained by German authorities around 1908, escaped, proclaimed she would drive out the Europeans, and was recaptured around 1911; she remained in detention near Kampala until the mid-1940s. Sources differ on details. This is a different history from Jamaican Nyabinghi, and how the name reached Jamaica is a separate, open question.

    Scholarly interpretationAfrica · Spirituality · Politics#link
  38. c. 1910s–1930s

    Jamaican heritage in Cuba

    After the Panama Canal work ends in the 1910s, many Jamaicans go to Cuba, drawn by sugar and United Fruit Company enclaves such as Banes. Roughly 200,000 Caribbean workers come to Cuba in the 1920s and early 1930s. Cuba encourages the expulsion of Antillean workers between 1925 and 1933, and repatriation follows in the 1930s. About 5,000 Jamaican descendants are said to remain, and Rita Marley and the writer Sylvia Wynter were born in Cuba to Jamaican families.

    Scholarly interpretationJamaica · Heritage · Music#link

    Not confirmed: the figures come from a dissertation cited secondhand, and some sources are blog-style.

    Source: Islas (Universidad Central de Las Villas): Jamaican migration to Cuba (Spanish; read only through a search summary)Ole Times Sumting: Having Cuba as a friend (blog)

  39. 1914

    Garvey founds the UNIA in Kingston

    Marcus Garvey founds the Universal Negro Improvement Association in Jamaica, building a Pan-African movement of self-determination that shapes the intellectual climate in which Rastafari later emerges.

    Documented historical factJamaica · Politics · Culture#link
  40. 1917–1931

    Chinese heritage: indenture ends, tension and limits

    Indenture is outlawed in 1917. A riot in 1918 beginning in Ewarton, linked to shopkeeper resentment and the opium trade, spreads to other parishes. From 1910 arrivals must pay a £30 deposit, and entry is restricted again in 1931.

    Scholarly interpretationJamaica · Heritage#link

    The 1918 riot account is flagged "citation needed" on Wikipedia.

    Source: Wikipedia: Chinese Jamaicans

  41. 2 Nov 1930

    Coronation of Haile Selassie I

    Ras Tafari Makonnen is crowned Emperor of Ethiopia. The event is historical fact. Its meaning to early Rastafari, who recognise Haile Selassie I as divine, is a matter of faith and is presented as belief, not as historical finding.

    Documented historical factRastafari · Spirituality · Africa#link

    Coronation: documented. Divinity: Rastafari belief.

  42. 1935

    Italy invades Ethiopia

    Fascist Italy invades Ethiopia. Ethiopia's resistance and Haile Selassie's appeal to the League of Nations resonate across the African diaspora, including Jamaica.

    Documented historical factAfrica · Politics · Rastafari#link
  43. c. 1940

    Leonard Howell establishes Pinnacle

    Early Rastafari preacher Leonard Percival Howell founds the Pinnacle community in St Catherine, one of the first large Rastafari settlements. Exact dates vary between sources.

    Scholarly interpretationRastafari · Jamaica · Spirituality#link
  44. 1943–1970s

    Chinese heritage in trade and music

    The 1943 census records 12,394 Chinese residents. By 1963 Chinese Jamaicans are reported to hold about 90 per cent of dry goods stores and 95 per cent of supermarkets. In the 1960s Vincent "Randy" Chin and Patricia Chin build the record shop and label that becomes VP Records. In the 1970s thousands leave amid inter-ethnic violence, mainly for Canada.

    Scholarly interpretationJamaica · Heritage · Music#link

    The shop figures are reported, not audited.

    Source: Wikipedia: Chinese Jamaicans

  45. 22 Jun 1948

    Windrush: heritage in Britain

    HMT Empire Windrush docks at Tilbury with a group of 802 migrants, and the British Nationality Act of 1948 gives people in the colonies citizenship and rights of entry. Jamaicans become part of a large Caribbean migration to Britain in the 1950s. Sound-system culture reaches the UK with Caribbean migration in the 1960s and 1970s, carrying the roots of UK reggae, dub and lovers rock.

    Documented historical factJamaica · Heritage · Music#link

    The Windrush article read gives 802 migrants and does not give the Jamaican share. The 1962 Commonwealth Immigrants Act restricted entry from the Commonwealth.

    Source: Wikipedia: Windrush generationWikipedia: Sound system (Jamaican)

  46. 1950s

    Mento and the sound system

    Mento is popular in Jamaica while sound-system operators in Kingston play American rhythm and blues to dance crowds. Selectors and producers such as Duke Reid and Coxsone Dodd begin to record local music, laying groundwork for ska.

    Documented historical factMusic · Jamaica · Culture#link

    Source: Smithsonian Folklife: Black History in Roots Reggae Music

  47. c. 1958

    Emanuel Charles Edwards and Bobo Ashanti

    Prince Emanuel Charles Edwards founds the Ethiopian African Black International Congress, the basis of the Bobo Ashanti house, centred at Bull Bay. Sources differ on the founding year.

    Scholarly interpretationRastafari · Spirituality · Jamaica#link
  48. c. 1959–61

    Count Ossie's drummers on record

    Nyabinghi-style drumming led by Count Ossie is heard on 'Oh Carolina' by the Folkes Brothers, produced by Prince Buster. It is an early documented meeting of Rastafari sacred drumming and Jamaican popular recording. Scholars point to Burru and Kumina as African-derived influences on Nyabinghi drumming; the exact lines of descent are interpretive.

    Scholarly interpretationMusic · Rastafari · Artists · Africa#link

    Source: Smithsonian Folklife: Black History in Roots Reggae Music

  49. 1961

    Jamaica sends a mission to Africa

    After a university report on the Rastafari movement, a delegation that includes Rastafari representatives, among them Mortimo Planno, travels to Ethiopia and other African countries.

    Documented historical factRastafari · Jamaica · Politics#link
  50. 6 Aug 1962

    Jamaican independence

    Jamaica becomes independent from Britain. Ska, the fast, off-beat dance music of Kingston studios, is often described as the sound of the new nation.

    Documented historical factJamaica · Politics · Music · Heritage#link
  51. April 1963

    Coral Gardens

    A violent incident near Montego Bay is followed by a roundup of Rastafari across Jamaica. In April 2017 the prime minister apologised in Parliament, calling the state's actions a grave injustice.

    Documented historical factRastafari · Jamaica · Politics#link
  52. 1960s onward

    Heritage in North America

    Nurses drive much Jamaican emigration to the US in the 1960s, and Toronto becomes the largest Jamaican community in Canada. The diaspora includes more than 1,100,000 people in the United States and 309,000 in Canada, by the figures given in Wikipedia.

    Scholarly interpretationJamaica · Heritage#link

    The 1965 US Immigration Act is widely cited as a turning point but is uncited in the article read; the figures are estimates.

    Source: Wikipedia: Jamaican diaspora

  53. 21 Apr 1966

    Haile Selassie visits Jamaica

    Tens of thousands greet the Emperor at Kingston's airport. The visit is a defining moment for the Rastafari movement and is later commemorated as Grounation Day.

    Documented historical factRastafari · Jamaica · Spirituality#link
  54. 1968

    Reggae named; Twelve Tribes of Israel founded

    'Do the Reggay' by Toots and the Maytals is often cited as the first record to use the word. In the same year Vernon Carrington, known as Prophet Gad, founds the Twelve Tribes of Israel. The two events are unrelated beyond date; Bob Marley's later association with the Twelve Tribes is documented, but his beliefs were not defined by one organisation.

    Documented historical factMusic · Rastafari · Artists#link
  55. 1973

    Catch a Fire reaches the world

    The Wailers release Catch a Fire on Island Records, introducing roots reggae to international audiences. Reggae is a collective achievement of many singers, players, producers and engineers; no single artist created it.

    Documented historical factMusic · Artists#link
  56. 1973

    Grounation

    Count Ossie and Cedric Brooks, with their groups merged as the Mystic Revelation of Rastafari, release the triple album Grounation, whose title piece runs over thirty minutes.

    Documented historical factMusic · Rastafari · Artists#link
  57. 1980

    Marley in Harare; Marley baptised

    Bob Marley performs at Zimbabwe's independence celebrations in April and, on 4 November, is baptised into the Ethiopian Orthodox Church as Berhane Selassie.

    Documented historical factArtists · Rastafari · Africa · Spirituality#link
  58. 1985

    'Sleng Teng' and the digital turn

    Wayne Smith's 'Under Mi Sleng Teng', produced by King Jammy, is built on a digital rhythm and reshapes dancehall production.

    Documented historical factMusic · Artists · Culture#link
  59. Feb–May 2004

    Haitian boat arrivals

    During the insurrection that led to the overthrow of Haiti's president, Haitian boat arrivals in Jamaica reach 538 by mid-May 2004, housed in facilities in Portland and St James.

    Documented historical factJamaica · Heritage#link

    A press report; the figure is a count given at the time.

    Source: Jamaica Observer, 16 May 2004: 47 more Haitian refugees arrive here

  60. 2006

    Jamaican Jewish Heritage Center

    The Jamaican Jewish Heritage Center opens to mark 350 years of Jews in Jamaica. In 2014 Chabad opens a second synagogue in Montego Bay. The 2012 census records 506 practising Jews.

    Documented historical factJamaica · Heritage#link

    Source: Wikipedia: History of the Jews in Jamaica

  61. Nov 2018

    UNESCO recognises reggae

    UNESCO inscribes the reggae music of Jamaica on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

    Documented historical factMusic · Culture · Jamaica#link
  62. Jul 2023–Oct 2024

    Haitians arriving by boat again

    A group of 37 Haitians lands in Portland on 10 July 2023. Between July 2023 and May 2024 five arrivals are recorded, and in October 2024, 17 Haitians reported to have been in Jamaica for about a year are returned home. The government says it has spent close to $30 million supporting Haitian migrants since July 2023.

    Documented historical factJamaica · Heritage#link

    Government and press figures; they may have changed since.

    Source: Global Voices (2023): Haitian refugees land on Jamaican shoresJamaica Observer, 23 Oct 2024: 17 Haitians returned home

  63. 3 May 2026

    180 years since the first Indians arrived

    The Indian High Commission unveils a renovated monument at Old Harbour Bay on 3 May 2026 to mark 180 years since the first East Indians arrived. Old Harbour is the focal point of Indian Heritage Day.

    Documented historical factJamaica · Heritage#link

    Source: The Gleaner, 4 May 2026: Jamaica marks 180 years of the arrival of East Indians

  64. Undated

    Not confirmed: Haitian, Cuban and Latin American communities

    Haitian, Cuban and Latin American communities are spoken of in Jamaica as part of the island's heritage. This is word of mouth. No dated Jamaican source has been confirmed for their settlement, so no dates or numbers are given here.

    Community oral traditionJamaica · Heritage#link

    Placeholder. Entries will be added when dated sources are found, for example from the Statistical Institute of Jamaica census reports or UN migrant-stock data.

ReggaeDNA

The Roots. The Rhythm. The Revolution.

A digital heritage project by African Identity, based in New York City, tracing reggae back through Jamaica to the African peoples, rhythms and ancestral traditions it grew from.

Every page is researched and written in our own words, with each claim labelled as fact, scholarship, belief or oral tradition, and disputed points flagged rather than smoothed over. Sources are listed on each page so claims can be checked.
