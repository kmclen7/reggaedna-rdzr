Archive · Twelve Tribes of Israel

Twelve tribes, twelve months.

The Twelve Tribes of Israel, a house of Rastafari founded in 1968 by Vernon Carrington (Prophet Gad), places each member in a tribe according to birth month. Each tribe carries a colour, a part of the body, an apostle and a quality. Below are the pairings as published in one secondary source, alongside the biblical names they borrow.

On "secret meanings". Nothing on this page is hidden or leaked. It is what the community’s teaching is publicly reported to say, plus the traditional biblical meanings of each name. We do not publish, and will not guess at, anything a community keeps private. The scheme here is the organisation’s belief and symbolism, not history, and members may hold it differently. The month table comes from a single published source.

Reuben

Strength

Name means

Body
Eyes
Apostle

In Genesis 49
Jacob's firstborn, called unstable as water and told he would lose first place.
The tribe in scripture

The firstborn who lost his privilege

Reuben was Jacob and Leah’s eldest son. His story includes both attempts to protect his family and a serious failure that cost him his standing.

  • Birth and mandrakes. Leah named him in hope that Jacob would love her. Later, Reuben brought his mother mandrakes, which became part of a dispute between Leah and Rachel. Genesis 29:32; 30:14–16
  • Violation of his father’s household. Reuben slept with Bilhah, Jacob’s concubine. Jacob later connected this act with Reuben’s loss of firstborn privilege. Genesis 35:22; 49:3–4; 1 Chronicles 5:1–2
  • Attempt to save Joseph. When his brothers planned to kill Joseph, Reuben persuaded them to put him in a pit, intending to rescue him later. He returned to find Joseph gone. Genesis 37:18–30
  • Responsibility for Benjamin. During the famine, Reuben offered his own sons’ lives as security for Benjamin’s return. Jacob refused. Genesis 42:37–38
  • Rebellion in the wilderness. Dathan and Abiram, descendants of Reuben, joined the rebellion against Moses. Numbers 16
  • Land east of the Jordan. Reuben and Gad requested grazing land outside Canaan proper. Moses agreed after they promised to help the other tribes fight for their inheritance. Numbers 32
  • The disputed altar. Reuben, Gad, and eastern Manasseh built an altar that other Israelites initially mistook for rebellion. They explained that it was a witness to their shared allegiance to God. Joshua 22
  • Failure to join Deborah’s battle. Deborah’s song describes Reuben’s divisions and hesitation while other tribes fought. Judges 5:15–16
  • Exile. Reuben was among the eastern tribes deported by Assyria. 1 Chronicles 5:18–26
May · Gold

Simeon

Faith

Name means
"Hearing"
Body
Ears
Apostle
Simon Peter
In Genesis 49
Joined with Levi in a blessing that speaks of anger, and of being scattered in Israel.
The tribe in scripture

Violence, scattering, and a smaller inheritance

Simeon was Jacob and Leah’s second son.

  • Birth. Leah understood his birth as evidence that God had heard her suffering. Genesis 29:33
  • Attack on Shechem. After Shechem violated their sister Dinah, Simeon and Levi deceived the city’s men into circumcision and then killed them. Jacob condemned their violence. Genesis 34
  • Held prisoner in Egypt. Joseph kept Simeon in custody while the brothers returned home to bring Benjamin. Genesis 42:18–24; 43:23
  • Jacob’s pronouncement. Jacob declared that Simeon and Levi would be scattered within Israel because of their anger and cruelty. Genesis 49:5–7
  • Zimri and the plague. Zimri, a Simeonite leader, publicly brought a Midianite woman into the camp during Israel’s unfaithfulness at Peor. Phinehas killed them, and the plague stopped. Numbers 25
  • Inheritance within Judah. Simeon received towns within Judah’s territory because Judah’s allotment was larger than it needed. Joshua 19:1–9
  • Joint campaigns with Judah. The two tribes cooperated in fighting the Canaanites. Judges 1:1–3, 17
  • Later expansion. Simeonite families sought pasture and conquered settlements near Gedor and in Mount Seir. 1 Chronicles 4:24–43
June · Purple

Levi

Will

Name means
"Joined, attached"
Body
Nose
Apostle
Matthew
In Genesis 49
Paired with Simeon in Genesis 49; later the tribe set apart for priestly service.
The tribe in scripture

From violence to sacred service

Levi was Leah’s third son. His descendants became Israel’s priests and other sanctuary workers.

  • Birth. Leah hoped his birth would deepen Jacob’s attachment to her. Genesis 29:34
  • Violence at Shechem. Levi joined Simeon in the killing described above. Jacob condemned both brothers. Genesis 34; 49:5–7
  • Moses, Aaron, and Miriam. All three belonged to Levi. Moses led Israel out of Egypt; Aaron became high priest; Miriam served as a prophetess. Exodus 2:1–10; 6:16–20; 15:20–21
  • The golden calf. When Moses called for those loyal to the Lord, the Levites gathered to him and carried out judgment within the camp. Exodus 32:25–29
  • Appointment to service. God appointed the Levites to serve at the tabernacle in place of Israel’s firstborn. Aaron’s family held the priesthood specifically. Numbers 3–4; 8
  • Korah’s rebellion. Korah, a Levite, challenged Moses and Aaron’s authority. Numbers 16–17
  • Phinehas’s zeal. Aaron’s grandson Phinehas acted during the Peor crisis and received a covenant of peace. Numbers 25:7–13
  • Cities instead of a tribal territory. The Levites received towns throughout Israel, including the cities of refuge. Numbers 35; Joshua 21
  • A Levite and the Danites. A Levite served at Micah’s private shrine and then became priest for migrating Danites, illustrating religious disorder. Judges 17–18
  • The Levite’s concubine. Her abuse and death at Gibeah led to civil war against Benjamin. Judges 19–21
  • Temple worship and reform. Levites served as musicians, gatekeepers, teachers, and assistants in worship. They also participated in later religious reforms. 1 Chronicles 23–26; 2 Chronicles 17; 29–31; 34–35
  • Return from exile. Priests and Levites helped restore worship and explain the Law to the people. Ezra 3; 8; Nehemiah 8–9
July · Brown

Judah

Praise

Name means
"Praise"
Body
Mouth and heart
Apostle
Judas
In Genesis 49
Compared to a lion's cub, with the sceptre promised to remain in his line.
The tribe in scripture

Repentance, kingship, and the messianic line

Judah was Leah’s fourth son and became the tribe associated with David’s royal house.

  • Birth and praise. Leah praised the Lord when Judah was born. Genesis 29:35
  • Sale of Joseph. Judah proposed selling Joseph rather than killing him. Genesis 37:26–28
  • Judah and Tamar. Judah failed to provide for his widowed daughter-in-law Tamar. After she exposed his wrongdoing, he acknowledged that she was more righteous than he was. Their son Perez became an ancestor of David. Genesis 38; Ruth 4:18–22
  • Offer to take Benjamin’s place. Judah later offered himself as a slave in Benjamin’s place. His appeal immediately preceded Joseph’s revelation of his identity. Genesis 44–45
  • Jacob’s blessing. Judah was compared to a lion and associated with rulership. Genesis 49:8–12
  • Leadership in the wilderness. Judah led the tribal marching order. Numbers 2:3–9; 10:14
  • Caleb’s faith. Caleb represented Judah among the spies and remained faithful when most of the others discouraged Israel. He later received Hebron. Numbers 13–14; Joshua 14
  • Achan’s sin. Achan, from Judah, took forbidden goods at Jericho, bringing judgment and defeat upon Israel. Joshua 7
  • The story of Ruth. Ruth joined a family from Bethlehem in Judah, married Boaz, and became David’s great-grandmother. Ruth 1–4
  • David and Solomon. David’s dynasty arose from Judah; Solomon built the temple in Jerusalem. 1 Samuel 16 onward; 2 Samuel; 1 Kings 1–11
  • The southern kingdom. After the monarchy divided, Judah became the principal tribe of the southern kingdom, alongside Benjamin. 1 Kings 12
  • Exile and return. Babylon destroyed Jerusalem and deported its people; returning exiles later rebuilt the temple and city. 2 Kings 24–25; Ezra; Nehemiah
  • Jesus’s ancestry. The New Testament identifies Jesus with Judah and David’s line. Matthew 1:1–16; Hebrews 7:14; Revelation 5:5

Issachar

Zeal

Thaddeus
In Genesis 49

The tribe in scripture

Labor, settlement, and discernment

Issachar was Jacob and Leah’s fifth son.

  • Birth. His birth followed the exchange involving Reuben’s mandrakes. Genesis 30:14–18
  • Jacob’s blessing. Issachar was compared to a strong donkey, with imagery of pleasant land, burdens, and labor. Genesis 49:14–15
  • Territorial inheritance. The tribe received land that included parts of the fertile Jezreel Valley. Joshua 19:17–23
  • Support for Deborah and Barak. Issachar’s leaders joined the struggle against Sisera. Judges 5:15
  • Tola the judge. Tola, an Issacharite, judged Israel for twenty-three years. Judges 10:1–2
  • Support for David. Issachar’s leaders were described as understanding the times and knowing what Israel should do. 1 Chronicles 12:32
  • Baasha’s dynasty. Baasha, from Issachar, seized the northern throne by killing Nadab. His dynasty later fell under judgment. 1 Kings 15:27–16:13

Zebulun

Order and compassion

Name means
"Dwelling"
Body

Apostle
James, son of Alpheus
In Genesis 49

The tribe in scripture

Courage in battle and Galilee’s promised light

Zebulun was Leah’s sixth son.

  • Birth. Leah hoped Jacob would honor her after she had borne him six sons. Genesis 30:19–20
  • Jacob’s blessing. Zebulun was associated with the sea, ships, and Sidon. Genesis 49:13
  • Inheritance. The tribe received territory in northern Israel. Joshua 19:10–16
  • Incomplete conquest. Some Canaanite inhabitants remained and were subjected to forced labor. Judges 1:30
  • Deborah and Barak. Zebulun joined Naphtali in the battle against Sisera and was praised for its courage. Judges 4:6–10; 5:14, 18
  • Gideon’s campaign. Zebulun answered the call to fight Midian. Judges 6:35
  • Elon the judge. Elon, a Zebulunite, judged Israel for ten years. Judges 12:11–12
  • Support for David. Zebulun supplied a large, organized force for David’s kingship. 1 Chronicles 12:33
  • Hezekiah’s Passover. Some people from Zebulun came to Jerusalem, although others mocked the invitation. 2 Chronicles 30:10–11
  • Jesus’s ministry. Matthew connects Jesus’s work in Galilee with Isaiah’s promise concerning Zebulun and Naphtali. Matthew 4:12–16

Dan

In Genesis 49

The tribe in scripture

Samson, migration, and idolatry

Dan was the son of Jacob and Rachel’s servant Bilhah.

  • Birth. Rachel named him in connection with God vindicating her. Genesis 30:1–6
  • Jacob’s blessing. Dan was associated with judging his people and compared to a serpent beside a road. Genesis 49:16–18
  • Tabernacle craftsmanship. Oholiab, a Danite, helped Bezalel make the tabernacle and its furnishings. Exodus 31:1–11
  • Difficulty holding territory. The Amorites confined Dan’s people to the hill country. Judges 1:34–35
  • Samson. The most famous Danite was Samson, whose extraordinary strength, conflicts with the Philistines, relationship with Delilah, capture, and final act of destruction occupy four chapters. Judges 13–16
  • Migration to Laish. Danite scouts found a new settlement in the north. The tribe captured Laish, renamed it Dan, and established a shrine using Micah’s stolen religious objects. Judges 17–18
  • Jeroboam’s golden calf. The city of Dan later became one of the northern kingdom’s two royal calf shrines. 1 Kings 12:26–30

Gad

Power

Name means
"Fortune" or "troop"
Body
Reproductive organs

In Genesis 49
Raided by a troop but promised to prevail in the end. The founder took this name, Prophet Gad.
The tribe in scripture

The warriors east of the Jordan

Gad was the son of Jacob and Leah’s servant Zilpah.

  • Birth. Leah named him in connection with good fortune. Genesis 30:9–11
  • Jacob’s blessing. Gad would be attacked by raiders but would strike back. Genesis 49:19
  • Eastern inheritance. Gad requested land suitable for livestock and agreed to help conquer Canaan before settling fully. Numbers 32
  • Fulfilled military obligation. Joshua released Gad, Reuben, and eastern Manasseh after their service. Joshua 22:1–9
  • The altar dispute. Gad participated in building the memorial altar that nearly provoked war among the tribes. Joshua 22:10–34
  • Support for David. Gadite warriors joined David while he was still a fugitive. 1 Chronicles 12:8–15
  • War and exile. Gad fought alongside the other eastern tribes but was later deported after unfaithfulness to God. 1 Chronicles 5:18–26

The prophet named Gad is a separate individual; his name alone does not establish membership in the tribe.

The tribe in scripture

Abundance and a faithful prophetic witness

Asher was Jacob’s second son by Zilpah.

  • Birth. Leah named him in connection with happiness. Genesis 30:12–13
  • Blessings of abundance. Jacob associated Asher with rich food, and Moses’s blessing used imagery of oil and security. Genesis 49:20; Deuteronomy 33:24–25
  • Incomplete conquest. Asher failed to drive out several Canaanite populations and lived among them. Judges 1:31–32
  • Absence from Deborah’s battle. Deborah’s song says Asher remained along the coast. Judges 5:17
  • Response to Gideon. Asher later joined Gideon’s summons against Midian. Judges 6:35; 7:23
  • Hezekiah’s Passover. Some from Asher humbled themselves and came to Jerusalem for the celebration. 2 Chronicles 30:11
  • Anna the prophetess. Anna, from Asher, recognized the significance of the infant Jesus and spoke about him to those awaiting Jerusalem’s redemption. Luke 2:36–38

In Genesis 49

The tribe in scripture

Northern battles and the land of Jesus’s ministry

Naphtali was Jacob’s second son by Bilhah.

  • Birth. Rachel connected his name with her struggle with her sister. Genesis 30:7–8
  • Jacob’s blessing. Naphtali was compared to a doe; translations differ on the wording that follows. Genesis 49:21
  • Barak and Deborah. Barak, from Kedesh in Naphtali, gathered troops from Naphtali and Zebulun to fight Sisera under Deborah’s direction. Judges 4
  • Courage in battle. Deborah’s song praises Naphtali and Zebulun for risking their lives. Judges 5:18
  • Support for Gideon. Naphtali joined the forces summoned against Midian. Judges 6:35; 7:23
  • Assyrian conquest. Naphtali’s territory was conquered and its people deported. 2 Kings 15:29
  • Prophecy and Jesus’s ministry. Isaiah’s promise of light for Zebulun and Naphtali is applied in Matthew to Jesus’s ministry in Galilee. Isaiah 9:1–2; Matthew 4:12–16
February · White

Joseph

Imagination

Name means
"May he add"
Body
Calves
Apostle
Bartholomew
In Genesis 49
A fruitful vine by a spring, whose blessing is the longest of the twelve. Bob Marley, born in February, is reported to belong to this tribe.
The tribe in scripture

Suffering, preservation, and two tribal inheritances

Joseph was Jacob’s first son by Rachel. His story explains how Israel’s family came to live in Egypt.

  • Birth. Rachel understood his birth as God removing her disgrace. Genesis 30:22–24
  • Favoritism and dreams. Jacob favored Joseph, and Joseph’s dreams increased his brothers’ hostility. Genesis 37:1–11
  • Betrayal. His brothers sold him, then deceived Jacob into believing he had been killed. Genesis 37:12–36
  • Slavery and imprisonment. Joseph served in Potiphar’s house, resisted Potiphar’s wife, and was imprisoned following her false accusation. Genesis 39
  • Interpretation of dreams. He interpreted the dreams of Pharaoh’s officials and later Pharaoh’s dreams about coming abundance and famine. Genesis 40–41
  • Rise to power. Pharaoh appointed him to oversee preparations for the famine. Genesis 41
  • Testing and reconciliation. Joseph tested his brothers, revealed his identity, forgave them, and brought the family to Egypt. Genesis 42–47
  • Ephraim and Manasseh adopted by Jacob. Jacob gave Joseph’s sons the standing of his own sons and placed Ephraim ahead of Manasseh in his blessing. Genesis 48
  • Final assurance. Joseph told his brothers that God had used their intended harm to preserve life. Genesis 50:15–21
  • His bones returned. Joseph requested burial in the promised land. Moses carried his bones out of Egypt, and they were eventually buried at Shechem. Genesis 50:24–26; Exodus 13:19; Joshua 24:32

Ephraim: Joshua and the northern kingdom

  • Joshua’s leadership. Joshua belonged to Ephraim and led Israel into Canaan. Numbers 13:8, 16; Joshua 1–24
  • Shiloh. The tabernacle was established at Shiloh in Ephraim’s territory. Joshua 18:1
  • Dispute with Gideon. Ephraim complained about being excluded from the initial battle against Midian; Gideon answered diplomatically. Judges 7:24–8:3
  • Conflict with Jephthah. A later dispute escalated into fighting and the fatal “Shibboleth” test at the Jordan crossings. Judges 12:1–6
  • Jeroboam’s rule. Jeroboam I was an Ephraimite and became the first king of the divided northern kingdom. 1 Kings 11:26; 12
  • Prophetic judgment and restoration. “Ephraim” often represents the northern kingdom in the prophets, especially Hosea. Hosea 4–14; Jeremiah 31

Manasseh: divided territory and notable families

  • Zelophehad’s daughters. Five sisters successfully requested an inheritance because their father had no sons. Their case established inheritance provisions for similar families. Numbers 27:1–11; 36; Joshua 17:3–6
  • Territory on both sides of the Jordan. Manasseh received eastern and western lands. Numbers 32:33–42; Joshua 17
  • Gideon. Gideon belonged to Manasseh. God called him to defeat Midian, ultimately using three hundred men. His later ephod became a religious snare. Judges 6–8
  • Abimelech. Gideon’s son killed his brothers and attempted to establish kingship, ending in violent judgment. Judges 9
  • Eastern Manasseh’s exile. The eastern half-tribe was deported with Reuben and Gad. 1 Chronicles 5:23–26

The tribe in scripture

Tragedy, Israel’s first king, and later faithful figures

Benjamin was Jacob and Rachel’s youngest son.

  • Birth and Rachel’s death. Rachel died giving birth to him. She named him Ben-oni, but Jacob called him Benjamin. Genesis 35:16–20
  • Joseph’s testing of the brothers. Benjamin’s journey to Egypt, Joseph’s special treatment of him, and the silver cup placed in his sack brought the brothers’ changed character into view. Genesis 42–45
  • Jacob’s blessing. Benjamin was compared to a ravenous wolf. Genesis 49:27
  • Ehud. The left-handed Benjaminite judge killed Eglon of Moab and delivered Israel from oppression. Judges 3:12–30
  • Civil war. Benjamin defended the men of Gibeah after the abuse and death of the Levite’s concubine. The resulting war nearly destroyed the tribe; the aftermath included further violence to obtain wives for the survivors. Judges 19–21
  • Saul. Israel’s first king came from Benjamin. His reign included early victories, disobedience, conflict with David, and death in battle. 1 Samuel 9–31
  • Jonathan. Saul’s son formed a covenant friendship with David and protected him despite his father’s hostility. 1 Samuel 18–20
  • Allegiance to Judah. Benjamin remained with the Davidic kingdom after the national division. 1 Kings 12:21
  • Mordecai and Esther. Mordecai is identified as a Benjaminite; Esther was his cousin and adopted daughter. Their story concerns the deliverance of the Jews in Persia. Esther 2–9
  • Paul. The apostle Paul explicitly identified himself as a member of Benjamin. Romans 11:1; Philippians 3:5

Another reading

Twelve names, read as a message.

Genesis 29 to 35 explains each name as something a mother said when her son was born. A Messianic Christian tradition notes that, read in birth order, the meanings can be heard as a short account of the gospel: a son is seen, heard, joined to, praised and judged, with the believer then wrestling, finding company, becoming glad, serving, dwelling and being added to.

This is a devotional reading. Its author cites Hebrews 4:2 and Galatians 3:8 for the idea that the good news was preached to Israel and to Abraham beforehand. It is a matter of faith and interpretation, not history, and it differs from the month, colour and quality scheme of the Rastafari Twelve Tribes house above.

Two cautions

  • The original article gives a second list in which Dan is missing and Manasseh appears. Manasseh was Joseph’s son, a grandson of Jacob, not one of the twelve sons, so this page uses the twelve sons only.
  • Hebrew name meanings in Genesis are explanations given in the story and may differ from a word-by-word translation. Benjamin was first named Ben-oni, "son of my sorrow", by his dying mother Rachel, and renamed by Jacob (Genesis 35:18).
